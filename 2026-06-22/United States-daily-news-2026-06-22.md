### China Bans 10 US Suppliers From Exports and 46 Defense Firms From Procurement After Pentagon Blacklist

China's Ministry of Commerce placed 10 American industrial suppliers on its export control list on Monday, June 22, 2026, and the Finance Ministry separately barred 46 U.S. companies — mostly defense contractors — from bidding on Chinese government procurement projects. The dual-track retaliation answered a June 9 move by the U.S. Pentagon, which added several Chinese technology companies, including Alibaba Group Holding Ltd. and BYD Co. Ltd., to its "1260H" list of firms it believes have aided Beijing's military.

The export ban hit rare earth miners MP Materials Corp. and USA Rare Earth Inc., drone makers Teal Drones and Jaia Robotics, electronics manufacturer Aveox Inc., Ball Aerospace & Technologies Corp., and Oshkosh Defense. It bars exports of any dual-use items originating in China to those companies. The procurement exclusion swept in larger primes: Lockheed Martin Corp., Raytheon Missiles & Defense, General Atomics Aeronautical Systems, Boeing Defense Space & Security, General Dynamics Land Systems, and Javelin Joint Venture. China's Ministry of Commerce said the controls were meant to safeguard national security and to answer what it called Washington's wrongful expansion of its list of Chinese military companies.

The targeting of MP Materials and USA Rare Earth pointed the dispute at firms Washington has bankrolled to build a domestic supply chain. MP Materials entered a public-private partnership with the Defense Department in 2025, and the Pentagon took a stake of roughly 15%. The 1260H designation imposes no immediate sanctions but bars the Defense Department from awarding direct contracts to affected Chinese entities starting June 30, with indirect-procurement curbs following in 2027. Han Shen Lin, China country director at consultancy The Asia Group, told CNBC the response looked "largely symbolic rather than a major escalation in U.S.-China tensions, since most of the targeted companies have little to no significant business in China."

### Fed Governor Waller Frames Dollar-Backed Stablecoins as Driver of Global Dollar Reach

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher J. Waller delivered welcoming remarks on June 22, 2026, opening the Fifth Conference on the International Roles of the U.S. Dollar, co-organized by the Federal Reserve Board and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in Washington, D.C. Waller said the traditional pillars supporting the dollar's global status — the depth and liquidity of U.S. financial markets, the strength of U.S. institutions, and the size of the American economy — remain foundational, but that "the environment around these drivers is changing rapidly."

He singled out the rise of distributed ledger technology and dollar-backed stablecoins as opening new channels for global dollar intermediation. "Technological innovation is increasingly altering how households and businesses interact with dollars," Waller said, adding that the private sector is moving quickly to widen access to dollar-denominated assets and to innovate in financial services. He voiced support for competition in the sector.

The two-day conference, running June 22–23, organized its agenda around four research questions: whether blockchain-based payment rails and stablecoin transactions can reshape cross-border payments; how stablecoin capital flows affect exchange rates and capital movements; how dollar-backed stablecoins link to demand for U.S. Treasury safe assets; and whether stablecoins reinforce or strain the international monetary system. The discussion reflected continued Fed attention to the GENIUS Act, enacted in July 2025, which established a federal stablecoin framework. Waller's framing cast stablecoins as a potential amplifier of dollar reach rather than a threat to it.

### Senate Invokes Cloture 84-8 on Bipartisan Housing Bill, Clearing Path to Final Passage

The U.S. Senate voted 84-8 around 11:00 a.m. on June 22, 2026, to invoke cloture on the motion to concur in the House Message accompanying H.R. 6644, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, with Scott Amendment #5823. Senators voting against were Armstrong, Cruz, Johnson, Lee, Paul, Scott (FL), Tuberville, and Young. A final vote on adopting the motion to concur — the bill's last Senate hurdle — was scheduled for about 5:30 p.m. the same day.

Co-authored by Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott (R-S.C.) and ranking member Elizabeth Warren (D-Mass.), the measure is pitched as the most significant federal housing affordability legislation in modern U.S. history. It provides $200 million annually from 2027 to 2031 in grants to states that adopt zoning reforms, and $100 million annually for commercial-to-residential conversion programs. It bars institutional investors controlling 350 or more single-family homes from buying additional ones, with civil penalties up to $1 million per violation, and grants renters a right of first refusal with a 30-day first-look period before sales. The bill also expands FHA mortgage limits and streamlines environmental reviews.

The House passed the bill 390-9 on February 9, 2026. The legislation takes effect 180 days after enactment and sunsets after 15 years. Law firm Latham & Watkins noted the bill would represent the most significant federal restriction on institutional investment in single-family housing in modern U.S. history.

### Poll Finds 49% of Americans Support Moratorium on New AI Data Center Construction

A poll by Milltown Partners — a consultancy that advises leading AI laboratories and technology startups — published by Axios on June 22, 2026, found data centers have become a stand-in for broader public anger at an AI future many Americans say they do not want. Only 38% of respondents said they would support a data center built near their home, while 34% would oppose it. More striking, 49% said they support a moratorium on construction of new data centers, against just 16% who oppose one.

The split suggests Americans are wary of the pace and terms of the buildout rather than categorically opposed. The survey polled 6,872 registered voters between May 10 and May 20, with a margin of error of three percentage points, and oversampled voters in Texas, Georgia, Michigan, California, and North Carolina — all states with active data center projects. Notably, only 8% of respondents who oppose data centers said they knew of one near their home, indicating the resistance is not chiefly rooted in direct experience.

The findings echo a March 2026 Gallup survey of 1,000 adults, published May 13, in which 70% of Americans opposed building AI data centers in their local area — 48% strongly — versus 26% in favor. That opposition rate topped resistance to local nuclear power plants, at 53%. Democrats opposed more intensely (56% strongly) than Republicans (39%). The pressure lands as the Big Five hyperscalers — Amazon, Alphabet/Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Oracle — have collectively committed $660–690 billion in capital expenditure for 2026, roughly 75% of it on AI infrastructure. The United States had 4,184 data centers as of April 2026, more than the next ten countries combined.

The Times has pursued the litigation while also striking licensing deals with technology companies.

### Former Fed Chairman Alan Greenspan, Who Steered the Economy Through Boom and Crisis, Dies at 100

Alan Greenspan, the longest-serving Federal Reserve chairman in U.S. history, died on Monday, June 22, 2026, at age 100, from complications of Parkinson's disease. His wife of 29 years, NBC News correspondent Andrea Mitchell, announced his death.

Greenspan was born March 6, 1926, in the Washington Heights section of New York City. He studied clarinet at the Juilliard School, then earned a bachelor's degree and a doctorate in economics from New York University. An early adherent of Ayn Rand's objectivist philosophy, he founded the economic consulting firm Townsend-Greenspan & Co. President Ronald Reagan appointed him the 13th chairman of the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System in August 1987, and he went on to serve under Presidents George H.W. Bush, Bill Clinton, and George W. Bush for a total of 18.5 years until January 31, 2006.

His tenure spanned the "Great Moderation," a stretch of low inflation and steady growth, and he steered the Fed through the October 1987 stock market crash, the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the bursting of the dot-com bubble, and the September 11, 2001 attacks. In December 1996 he coined the phrase "irrational exuberance" to warn of overvalued markets. Critics later argued his backing of financial deregulation and low interest rates in the early 2000s helped set up the 2007–08 global financial crisis. Testifying before Congress in 2008, Greenspan conceded, "I have found a flaw," in his regulatory ideology. He married Andrea Mitchell in 1997.

### 500 Concessions Workers Strike at Target Field During Twins-Dodgers Game

About 500 concessions workers at Target Field in Minneapolis walked off the job on June 22, 2026, during the Minnesota Twins' home game against the Los Angeles Dodgers, in the first strike ever at a major league stadium in Minnesota. The workers are represented by Unite Here Local 17 and employed by Delaware North, the stadium's concessions operator. Union members had authorized the one-day action on May 1 by an 81% vote, and workers walked out at 4 p.m. ahead of the evening game.

The union asked fans to bring their own food and not cross the picket line. Delaware North said in a statement it was its full intention to continue full-service concessions at Target Field should there be a strike. The Dodgers came in as the reigning World Series champions.
