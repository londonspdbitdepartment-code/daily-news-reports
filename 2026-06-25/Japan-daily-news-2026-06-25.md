### BOJ hawk Tamura sketches path of quarter-point hikes every few months to a 2% neutral rate

Bank of Japan Policy Board member Naoki Tamura told business leaders in Hyogo Prefecture on June 25 that his baseline path is "raising the policy interest rate by 0.25 percentage points at intervals of a few months toward the neutral interest rate level of 2%." Tamura, the board's most consistent hawk, was speaking nine days after the BOJ lifted its policy rate to 1.0% at the June 16 meeting — the highest setting since September 1995, the tail end of Japan's bubble era.

His argument rests on a claim few of his colleagues have made out loud: "underlying inflation has already reached 2%," with the risks now tilted firmly to the upside. He pointed to the energy shock from the Iran war, a tight labour market, and a weak yen passing higher import costs through to prices faster than after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine. If those upside risks build further, Tamura said, "it's necessary to accelerate the pace of rate hikes without hesitation by increasing the frequency or size of rate hikes."

That is a more aggressive cadence than the market expects. A Reuters poll has most economists penciling in the next move — to 1.25% — only by the fourth quarter. The next policy meeting falls on July 30–31. Working against Tamura is the Takaichi government, which prefers cheaper borrowing to fund its spending plans, leaving the board to tighten into political headwinds.

### Takaichi's ¥370 trillion investment blueprint reignites doubts over Japan's bond market

Bond strategists warned on June 25 that Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's ¥370 trillion ($2.3 trillion) investment strategy, unveiled the previous day, is feeding fresh anxiety about where Japan's government debt market is heading. The plan sets a benchmark for combined public and private spending across 17 strategic sectors by FY2040, and its scale is the point of contention: the government is signalling a multi-decade commitment to outspend even as the BOJ trims its monthly JGB purchases.

The sector map reads like an industrial-policy wish list. AI and semiconductors carry the largest tag at ¥68 trillion; data centres and storage batteries draw ¥32.7 trillion by FY2035; content and entertainment ¥30 trillion, with ¥24.5 trillion of that for games; biopharmaceuticals ¥20.8 trillion; physical AI ¥10.5 trillion; autonomous vehicles ¥8.2 trillion; and nuclear fusion ¥3 trillion. The strategy lists 62 designated products and technologies and targets nearly 2% GDP growth by FY2040. The government's own estimates are bullish — it projects AI investment alone could generate ¥443 trillion in economic impact. Takaichi has framed the programme as "responsible and proactive fiscal spending."

The market's verdict came quickly. On June 25, the 20-year JGB auction drew its weakest demand since the May 2025 bond rout, with a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.97 against a 12-month average of 3.55 — a direct read on investor unease over a balance sheet already among the most stretched in the developed world.

### Japan's 20-year bond sale draws weakest demand in over a year as fiscal worries pile up

Japan's Ministry of Finance saw the bid-to-cover ratio on its June 25 20-year JGB auction fall to 2.97, the lowest since the May 2025 market rout and well under both the 12-month average of 3.55 and the previous sale's 4.01. The drop is the clearest signal yet that the buyer base for long-dated Japanese debt is thinning at precisely the moment the government is promising to spend more.

Three forces are compounding. The Iran war's energy shock is feeding inflation; the BOJ's rate path is uncertain after its June 16 increase to 1.0%; and Takaichi's ¥370 trillion, 14-year investment programme, announced June 24, adds to the supply outlook. Domestic anchor buyers are stepping back — Japanese insurers cut JGB holdings in May, and international managers are paring long-duration exposure.

The retreat is reshaping where banks raise money. Regional lenders, squeezed by rising competition for deposits, are turning to the bond market: Yokohama Financial Group priced its inaugural deal on June 25, ¥12 billion of three-year notes at 27 basis points over JGBs, with Shiga Bank and Shizuoka Financial Group lining up ¥20 billion and ¥60 billion offerings in July.

### Yen near a 40-year low is set to gift Japan's carmakers a $5.8 billion profit windfall

The yen hovered around 161.5–161.8 per dollar on June 25, its weakest since 1986, even after Japanese authorities burned through a record ¥11.73 trillion ($73 billion) between late April and late May trying to arrest the slide. Bloomberg estimates that Toyota, Honda, Nissan and their peers stand to pocket a combined earnings upside of roughly ¥934 billion ($5.8 billion) this year if the currency stays near these levels, as the weak yen inflates the home-currency value of their overseas revenue.

The windfall is the flip side of a national problem. The same currency that pads exporter profits is driving up the cost of imported fuel and food, squeezing households already worn down by Japan's prolonged inflation. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama has repeated warnings of "bold action" against excessive moves, but markets have grown skeptical after the spring intervention drew down reserves without holding the line — the yen breached 161 again within weeks.

The root cause is structural and stubborn: even after the BOJ's June hike to 1.0%, the gap to US rates above 5% keeps capital flowing out of the yen. Until that spread narrows, verbal warnings carry limited weight.

## 2. Politics & Diplomacy

### LDP proposes cutting Japan's food consumption tax to 1% for two years from April 2027

Itsunori Onodera, who chairs the ruling Liberal Democratic Party's tax commission, unveiled an interim draft on June 25 to cut the consumption tax on food and beverages from 8% to 1% for two years starting April 2027. The plan falls short of the zero-percent food tax Takaichi promised during her February campaign — a gap chosen for speed, not principle. Retailers need about five to six months to reprogram cash registers for a 1% rate, against ten to twelve months for a clean zero.

To bridge that gap, the draft pairs the cut with cash. The roughly ¥600 billion in revenue still collected at 1% would be returned as income-tested benefits to lower- and middle-income households, effectively zeroing out their food tax burden, with extra payments for families with children up to age 15. The cash handouts are slated to begin around fall, with a fuller income-linked benefit system targeted for 2029. The total annual cost runs to about ¥5 trillion.

The council wants the measures formalised within June, but consensus is shaky. "Gaps between parties remain quite large," Onodera said. "It would be difficult to obtain consent in a certain direction at the next meeting." The final call rests with Takaichi.

### Ruling coalition submits second-capital bill to the Diet in a race against the July 17 deadline

The LDP and its junior partner the Japan Innovation Party submitted a bill to the House of Representatives on June 24 to create a "second capital" — a designated major city that would take over Tokyo's core government functions if a large-scale disaster crippled the capital. The legislation is a deliverable from the October 2025 coalition agreement, the deal under which JIP leader Hirofumi Yoshimura threw his party behind Sanae Takaichi's election as Japan's first female prime minister.

The bill arrived stripped of its most contentious feature. An original provision allowing a prefecture-wide referendum on reorganising Osaka into a metropolis was cut after LDP resistance; the final text limits any referendum to voters inside the relevant city. Yoshimura, who has long championed the Osaka metropolis project, still plans a third referendum on it in spring 2027.

Time is short. The two parties are pushing to clear the bill before the Diet session ends July 17. Letting it lapse would mark a breach of the coalition pact that put Takaichi in office.

### US has held just one Pacific carrier for 22 weeks, deepening Japan's security anxieties

The US military has kept only one aircraft carrier in the Pacific since January 2026 — half its customary two — for 22 straight weeks as of June 25, a Nikkei Asia investigation found. The drawdown predates the Iran war and reflects the Trump administration's reallocation of naval forces toward the Middle East and the Western Hemisphere. The lone remaining carrier is based at Yokosuka in Japan.

The timing unsettles Tokyo. China has been expanding its fleet, having launched its third aircraft carrier, shifting the regional balance just as US presence thins near Japan's southwestern islands, where Chinese activity around Taiwan and in the East China Sea has intensified. Japan is racing to fill part of the gap itself, converting its two Izumo-class helicopter destroyers — JS Izumo by FY2027 and JS Kaga by FY2028 — into carriers able to operate F-35B jets. It has also been waiting roughly five years for $7.2 billion in undelivered US military equipment.

Nikkei framed the strategic bind plainly: "With China continuing its military buildup and deploying aircraft carriers in the Pacific Ocean, U.S. President Donald Trump's newly sharpened focus on the Western Hemisphere leaves Japan urgently needing to bolster multilateral cooperation to complement its defense pact with America."

## 3. Technology & Industry

### Japan's ground forces ran China-linked malware on classified computers for nearly a year

Japan's Ground Self-Defense Force used counterfeit USB memory sticks carrying malware tied to Chinese hacking groups on computers with access to classified military information for about a year, a Nikkei investigation published June 25 revealed. The infected drives contained malware associated with MirrorFace, a subgroup of the China-linked APT10 threat actor.

The exposure sits inside a broader campaign. Japan's National Police Agency and the National center of Incident readiness and Strategy for Cybersecurity have linked MirrorFace to more than 200 cyber incidents targeting the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the Ministry of Defense, the space agency JAXA, semiconductor firms and aerospace companies. What makes the SDF case worse is the response: even after discovering the infected drives resembled counterfeit sticks sold openly online in Japan — a fact that widened the potential blast radius — the force chose not to disclose the breach.

The episode lands weeks before a shift in Japan's cyber posture. From October 1, 2026, the SDF will be authorised to conduct offensive cyber operations against attacking infrastructure under the country's new Active Cyber Defense Law.

### Honda, Nissan and Mitsubishi near a deal to standardise EV control units against Chinese rivals

Honda, Nissan and Mitsubishi Motors entered final talks on June 25 to standardise the electronic control units (ECUs) in their next-generation battery-electric and hybrid vehicles. ECUs are the core computers that run autonomous-driving features, powertrain management and safety systems in software-defined cars — and standardising them lets the three buy in volume and share development costs.

The move is a defensive one. Japanese carmakers have fallen behind Chinese EV brands and Tesla in software architecture, where centralised computing and over-the-air updates have become the competitive front line. By pooling procurement, Honda, Nissan and Mitsubishi aim to claw back some of that cost disadvantage; vehicles using the standardised units could reach showrooms in 2029–2030. Honda and Nissan are separately discussing standardising their automotive operating systems and cooperating on production in the United States.

The talks extend a wider pattern of integration among the alliance partners as they confront the expensive shift from mechanical engineering to software-centric vehicles.

## 4. Society & Livelihood

### Magnitude 7.2 quake strikes off northeastern Japan, injuring four and halting bullet trains

A magnitude 7.2 earthquake — initially reported as 6.9, then upgraded by the Japan Meteorological Agency — struck off the Pacific coast of Iwate Prefecture at around 7:30 a.m. on June 25, at a depth of roughly 44–50 kilometres. It registered upper 6 on Japan's seven-point intensity scale in Hashikami and lower 6 in Hachinohe, both in Aomori Prefecture. Four people suffered minor injuries — teenagers and people in their 50s. No tsunami warning was issued, and the four nuclear facilities checked, including Fukushima Daiichi, reported no abnormalities.

The quake disrupted the morning routine across the northeast. Shinkansen services between Tokyo and Shin-Aomori were halted, with the Tokyo–Sendai stretch resuming by 9:30 a.m. Five schools closed in Hashikami and 65 in Hachinohe; mild shaking reached Tokyo during rush hour. The JMA's description of an upper-6 zone is stark: "It is impossible to remain standing or move without crawling, and most unfixed furniture will move and objects will topple over."

Prime Minister Takaichi activated an emergency response team at the Crisis Management Center. "Under the principle of prioritizing human life above all else, spare no effort in our emergency disaster responses," she said, urging residents to "remain alert for further earthquakes of similar intensity."

### A5 wagyu glut pushes Japanese producers abroad as inflation-hit consumers shun luxury beef

Top-grade A5 wagyu — Japan's highest marbling rating — now makes up over 75% of the domestic beef market, creating a severe glut, according to a Nikkei Asia investigation published June 25. Decades of breeding incentives steered the national herd toward premium grades, but the buyers have thinned out: inflation-weary Japanese consumers are increasingly walking past expensive luxury cuts.

The mismatch is forcing producers overseas, where they end up competing against each other on price in export markets. It is a slow-burn consequence of agricultural policy colliding with a changed economy — a weak yen lifting import costs and the Iran war's energy shock have together eroded real disposable incomes, leaving households with less room for indulgence even as the supply of marbled beef keeps coming.

The result is a grading structure built for an affluent, growth-era consumer landscape that no longer exists, and a premium product the home market can no longer absorb.

Fuji Media is shedding its real estate arm as part of a restructuring forced by a year of governance scrutiny and shareholder activism at the owner of Fuji Television. The contest underscores how aggressively foreign private equity is chasing Japanese corporate carve-outs and prime real estate.

### Cool Japan Fund posts ¥54 billion in losses as ministry weighs scrapping the cultural-export vehicle

The Cool Japan Fund reported accumulated losses of ¥54 billion ($334 million) in results released June 25, blowing past its internal cap of ¥42.6 billion and reviving questions about whether the state-backed vehicle should survive at all. Launched in 2013 under Prime Minister Shinzo Abe to push anime, cuisine and fashion abroad as growth drivers, the fund has instead become a case study in public investment gone wrong, with the red ink stemming mainly from poor domestic startup bets.

The numbers are large. The government had put in ¥140.6 billion and private companies ¥10.7 billion, for ¥151.3 billion total as of March 2026. Japanese rules trigger a mandatory review when a public-private fund misses its cumulative target three or more times — the Cool Japan Fund already missed in FY2020 and FY2021, and this latest overshoot pushes it firmly into that territory.

The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry will set up an investigative panel in July to decide the fund's fate, with options including abolition or merger and a verdict expected by year-end.

### Hoshinoya opens Japan's first luxury hotel inside a Meiji-era former prison in Nara

Hoshino Resorts opened Hoshinoya Nara Prison on June 25 inside a red-brick complex completed in 1908 as one of five major penitentiaries built during Japan's Meiji Era. The property carved 48 suites out of former solitary and shared cells across four of the prison's five radially arranged wings, keeping original details intact — exposed brick walls, high-set windows — while reworking the interiors into minimalist rooms. One-night stays start at 147,000 yen.

The building's afterlife is unusual. The only fully intact survivor of Japan's five largest Meiji-era prisons, it later served as a juvenile detention centre before closing in 2017 due to ageing infrastructure, and was designated a National Important Cultural Property that same year. Guests can visit the on-site Nara Prison Museum, which opened separately on April 27. The hotel's debut had been planned for 2024 but slipped while the structure underwent earthquake-resistance assessment.

General manager Masaya Kakegawa said the project aims to "pass on the history and value of this building to future generations," and targets 80% occupancy within three years. The complex sits near Nara Park and the Todai-ji temple.

### Japan's Moriyasu vows to beat Sweden and top the group at the World Cup, eyeing an escape from Brazil

Japan manager Hajime Moriyasu told reporters in Dallas on June 25 that his side's only goal was to beat Sweden in their final Group F match and finish first. "As for our objectives, we want to win and qualify in first place," he said. Japan and the Netherlands were level on four points and goal difference heading into their final games; a Japan win paired with a Dutch win over already-eliminated Tunisia would send Japan through as group winners.

The stakes are about who comes next, not just survival. Topping the group sets up a Round of 32 meeting with Morocco in Monterrey, where Japan trained earlier in the tournament; finishing second means a clash with five-time champion Brazil in Houston. Moriyasu sized up the opponent carefully, calling Sweden "a very strong side — solid in defence and quick in attack," and flagging Premier League strikers Alexander Isak and Viktor Gyokeres as threats despite Sweden's 5-1 loss to the Netherlands.

History looms over the match. Japan have reached the knockout stage four times but never passed the Round of 16, most recently losing to Croatia on penalties in 2022 — though their 4-0 win over Tunisia this tournament made them the first Asian team to score four goals in a single World Cup game.
