### Eurozone composite PMI reaches 50.0 in June as services contraction eases and inflation cools

The S&P Global/HCOB Eurozone Composite PMI climbed to 50.0 in June 2026, the reading that separates growth from decline, ending two straight months below the line. Released on 3 July 2026, the final figure was an upward revision from the flash estimate of 49.5 and a clear step up from 48.5 in May. Anything at 50 signals an economy that has stopped shrinking without yet expanding, but the composition of the improvement told the more useful story.

Services did most of the lifting. The services PMI rose to 49.4 from 47.7 in May, above the preliminary estimate of 48.9, while manufacturing output held its ground at 51.4 against May's 51.3. The turn in services matters because it accounts for the bulk of eurozone output and employment, and firms there added staff at the fastest pace since January 2026.

Cost pressures collapsed. Survey respondents reported services input cost inflation falling at the steepest rate since records began in 1998, barring only the COVID-19 lockdowns. Cheaper energy was the driver, as easing tension over the Strait of Hormuz pulled oil prices back. That fed directly into the wider price picture: eurozone annual inflation stood at 2.8% in June, according to Eurostat's flash estimate on 1 July, well below the 3.2% recorded in May and back within touching distance of the European Central Bank's 2% target. National divergence persisted, with Italy, Spain and Ireland above 50 while Germany and France stayed in contraction, though both moderated.

### ECB reports eurozone current account surplus shrank to €275 billion in year to Q1 2026

The European Central Bank published the euro area quarterly balance of payments for Q1 2026 on 3 July 2026, showing the bloc's four-quarter cumulative current account surplus falling to €275 billion, or 1.7% of GDP, from €352 billion, or 2.3%, a year earlier. The current account tracks the gap between what the eurozone earns abroad and what it pays out, and a narrower surplus means that cushion is thinning.

The retreat came almost entirely from trade. The goods surplus contracted from €344 billion to €313 billion, and the services surplus declined from €169 billion to €149 billion. Income flows barely moved, with the primary income surplus edging from €11 billion to €10 billion, while the secondary income deficit, which captures transfers such as remittances and EU budget contributions, widened from €171 billion to €197 billion.

The bilateral breakdown underscored the eurozone's shifting trade map. Its largest surplus was recorded with the United Kingdom at €236 billion; its largest deficit was with China at €170 billion. On the stock side, the eurozone's net external assets rose to €1.80 trillion, or 11.2% of GDP, an increase of €147 billion from the €1.65 trillion booked in Q4 2025, while gross external debt reached €17.53 trillion, equivalent to 109% of eurozone GDP.

### Eurostat says euro area household saving rate held at 14.3% in Q1 2026 as corporate profits slipped

Households in the euro area kept 14.3% of their disposable income aside in the first quarter of 2026, unchanged from the previous quarter, according to the first quarterly sector accounts Eurostat published on 3 July 2026. The stability is telling: household consumption and gross disposable income grew at roughly the same rate, 0.8% and 0.7% respectively, so families spent their income gains rather than banking or drawing down more of them. The dataset now covers the EA21, including Bulgaria as of January 2026.

Behind the steady headline, the balance between households and business shifted. The household investment rate fell slightly from 8.6% to 8.5%, while the corporate profit share declined from 39.7% to 38.6%. Companies leaned into capital spending regardless, with the non-financial corporate investment rate rising from 21.7% to 22.2% even as their gross value added contracted by 0.9% — a squeeze on margins met by continued outlay rather than retrenchment.

A separate Eurostat release the same day put the EU's seasonally adjusted current account surplus at €113.4 billion in Q1 2026, or 2.4% of GDP, up from €99.2 billion in Q4 2025 and €104.9 billion in Q1 2025. Germany led member states with a €61.8 billion surplus, while the largest bilateral deficit was with China at €66.3 billion.

### EU Commission adopts revised ESRS, cutting mandatory reporting datapoints by more than 60%

The European Commission formally adopted revised European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) on 3 July 2026, stripping out more than 60% of the mandatory datapoints and more than 70% of total datapoints compared with the 2023 standards. Per-company reporting costs are expected to fall by more than 30%. The overhaul is the centrepiece of the Omnibus I simplification package, the drive launched in February 2025 to lighten the compliance load that businesses and several member governments had argued was blunting EU competitiveness.

The revised standards still cover climate change, biodiversity and human rights, but ask for far less granular disclosure. Alongside them the Commission introduced a new voluntary reporting standard for firms that fall outside the scope of the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) — the smaller companies that Omnibus I also removed from mandatory reporting when it raised thresholds to firms with more than 1,000 employees. A "value chain cap" reinforces that protection by barring CSRD-scope companies from demanding more information from their suppliers than the voluntary standard requires.

The rewrite incorporates technical advice from EFRAG, the Commission's standards adviser, following a spring 2025 stakeholder consultation. As delegated acts, the revised standards now go to the European Parliament and Council of the EU for a 2-month scrutiny period, extendable by a further 2 months, before they can take effect.

### Moldova's prime minister Munteanu resigns after eight months, triggering government collapse

Alexandru Munteanu, Moldova's prime minister since November 2025, resigned on 3 July 2026, saying he could no longer govern in accordance with his principles and convictions. Under Moldovan rules his departure automatically pulled down the entire cabinet, ending a government that had been in post for approximately eight months. Munteanu, an economist and former World Bank official, had been installed after the pro-European Party of Action and Solidarity (PAS) of President Maia Sandu won the September 2025 parliamentary elections, taking a clear majority in a vote Sandu had called the most important in the country's history and one marked by documented Russian interference.

The collapse arrived at an awkward moment. Munteanu's exit came amid scrutiny of alleged irregularities at the state-owned air navigation service provider MoldATSA, including an executive with a falsified CV and a presidential cousin appointed to a public relations role at a salary eight times Moldova's median wage. President Sandu pushed back on suggestions the premier had been hamstrung, insisting "The Prime Minister had a free hand to lead the government as he saw fit."

The stakes run beyond one cabinet. Moldova's EU accession process was formally launched only weeks earlier, on 15 June 2026, with the opening of accession talks in Luxembourg. Sandu moved to reassure Brussels and voters alike, declaring "Moldova's path remains unchanged: reforms and EU accession," and said she would hold consultations with parliamentary groups next week. Munteanu stays on in a caretaker capacity until a new government is formed.

### EU data protection rules delayed 11% of LLM releases versus the US, GovAI study finds

A report by the Centre for the Governance of AI (GovAI), published on 3 July 2026, found that EU data protection rules — primarily the GDPR — delayed or blocked at least 11% of advanced large language model releases in the European Union compared with the United States, and 7% compared with the United Kingdom. The analysis examined 375 LLMs released over eight years, from June 2018 to May 2026, by companies including Meta, Google, OpenAI and Anthropic. Of 68 documented cases where a model was delayed or withheld in the EU, 56 were attributed to regulatory factors.

The burden fell unevenly. Meta faced the highest impact, with 26% of its EU releases delayed or withheld against 15% in the UK, and models handling non-text data such as images and audio ran into greater obstacles than text-only systems. Among individual products, Anthropic's Claude 3 Opus experienced a 71-day delay in its EU web application launch. GovAI judged EU enforcement more aggressive than the UK's, a gap that could widen: the EU AI Act's full applicability deadline is 2 August 2026, layering a second regulatory regime on top of the GDPR.

The findings feed a live argument in Brussels over whether Europe's caution comes at the cost of access. "It's important that policymakers consider delays to model access as a factor when implementing and designing AI-related regulations," said John Lidiard, a UK AI policy researcher.

### Citizen Lab confirms EU lawmaker who investigated Pegasus was himself hacked with Pegasus

The Citizen Lab at the University of Toronto published a forensic research report on 3 July 2026 revealing that Stelios Kouloglou, a Greek former Member of the European Parliament (2015–2024) and investigative journalist, was infected with NSO Group's Pegasus spyware at least three times while serving on the European Parliament's PEGA Committee — the body specifically established in 2022 to investigate illegal spyware use across EU member states. It is the first publicly identified case of a PEGA member compromised while doing the committee's work.

Forensic analysis of Kouloglou's iPhone dated the intrusions to on or around 21 October 2022 and two more on 6–7 March 2023, all using the zero-click PWNYOURHOME exploit, which requires no action from the target. The committee had been set up in the wake of Europe's spyware scandals, including Greece's "Predatorgate" affair, in which journalists and opposition politicians were surveilled. That the watchdog's own members were being watched sharpens those findings considerably; researchers noted the attackers could have reached confidential documents and committee deliberations.

Citizen Lab found no evidence attributing the attack to the Greek government, and noted an overlap with an earlier campaign targeting Russian and Belarusian-speaking exiled journalists and activists in Europe. "Spyware doesn't make democracies safer. It weakens democratic oversight, parliamentary independence and the rule of law," said MEP Hannah Neumann.

Preliminary mortality data published 3 July 2026 attributed more than 4,000 excess deaths across Western Europe to the late-June heatwave. Belgium recorded 1,222 additional deaths, a 39% increase between 18 and 29 June 2026, described as the highest daily death toll in the country since the first wave of the coronavirus.

The scale of the loss has spilled into politics. French opposition figures filed a no-confidence motion over what they called inadequate preparation for rising temperatures — a charge that reframes heat deaths as a question of governance and infrastructure rather than an unavoidable act of nature.

### Eurostat releases 2025 Dublin data showing 111,708 asylum transfer requests, down 26% on 2024

EU member states submitted a total of 111,708 outgoing requests in 2025 to determine which country was responsible for examining an asylum claim, a 26% decrease from 151,830 in 2024, according to Eurostat data published on 3 July 2026. Germany was the largest sender of outgoing requests with 35,933, while Latvia sent the fewest with 23. Across the bloc, 115,625 incoming requests were received, a 31% decrease from the prior year, with Italy receiving the most incoming requests, approximately 24,152, and Estonia the fewest at 88.

The gap between requests and results remained wide. Although paper requests fell sharply, actual transfers barely moved: 16,620 outgoing transfers were carried out, a 1% decrease from 2024's 16,459, and 17,406 incoming transfers were implemented, a 1% increase. The persistent gulf between requests and completed transfers was one of the central criticisms of the system these numbers describe.

The data lands three weeks after the New Pact on Migration and Asylum entered into application on 12 June 2026, replacing the Dublin system. Its successor, the Asylum and Migration Management Regulation, keeps the first-country-of-entry principle but adds a mandatory but flexible solidarity mechanism, under which states unwilling to relocate asylum seekers can instead pay €20,000 per person not accepted.

### Scientists confirm Europe's extreme heatwaves now tens to hundreds of times more likely than in 2003

Scientists from World Weather Attribution have confirmed that intense European heatwaves are now tens to hundreds of times more likely than they were in 2003 — the year a heatwave killed approximately 70,000 people across Europe and reset the continent's sense of what summer could do. The reason is straightforward: Europe has warmed at roughly twice the global average since the 1980s, so the temperatures that once counted as freak events now sit much closer to the middle of the distribution.

The June 2026 heatwave showed how far the baseline has shifted. Germany, the Czech Republic and Poland reached 40°C (104°F), while France averaged 29.8°C with spikes to 44°C. "Think of it like a race where the starting line has been moved much closer to the finish," said Dr. Akshay Deoras of the University of Reading, describing how warming makes each new record easier to break.

The mortality trend runs in the same direction. The Lancet Countdown Europe's 2026 report recorded 62,000 heat-related deaths across Europe in 2024, and found that 823 of 826 monitored European regions — 99.6% — saw increased heat-related deaths compared with 1991–2000. "Heat-related mortality is likely to remain a feature of Europe's warming climate," said Dr. Hans Kluge, WHO Europe Director. Professor Hannah Cloke, also of the University of Reading, tied the outlook back to policy: "Every tonne of emissions avoided changes the odds of what comes next."

The result is a market putting more money to work through steadier, larger commitments.
