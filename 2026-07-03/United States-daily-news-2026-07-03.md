### US payrolls add just 57,000 in June as unemployment slips to 4.2%

US employers added 57,000 jobs in June, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported, a figure the agency released a day early on July 2 ahead of the July 4 holiday. The unemployment rate edged down to 4.2% from 4.3%, but the composition told a weaker story: the drop owed partly to a labor force participation rate that fell 0.3 percentage points to 61.5%, as workers left the count rather than found work.

Hiring was concentrated in a handful of sectors. Professional and business services added 36,000 jobs, social assistance 25,000, and health care 22,000. Leisure and hospitality shed 61,000 — a sharp contraction heading into the summer travel season — while government payrolls rose 8,000. The BLS also revised the two prior months lower, cutting April by 31,000 (from 179,000 to 148,000) and May by 43,000 (from 172,000 to 129,000), erasing 74,000 jobs from the earlier tally.

Wage growth held up. Average hourly earnings rose 13 cents, or 0.3%, to $37.64, up 3.5% over the year. Acting Secretary of Labor Sonderling issued a statement accompanying the release.

### Weak jobs and cooling oil harden expectations of a prolonged Fed pause

The 57,000 June payroll gain landed at half the 115,000 that Dow Jones-polled economists had forecast, and traders read it as confirmation the Federal Reserve has no reason to raise rates this year. May was revised down to 129,000, unemployment slipped to 4.2% from 4.3%, and labor force participation fell to 61.5% — the lowest since March 2021. The report arrived after months in which some officials had flagged renewed inflation pressure, driven largely by a spike in gasoline prices tied to the February war with Iran that pushed inflation to a three-year high.

That inflation impulse is now easing. Oil traded at $67.20, and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who has said prices remain too high, held the benchmark rate at 3.50%-3.75% at his first meeting in June without signaling cuts. The soft jobs print pushed a September hike off the table in futures markets. Treasuries barely moved: the 2-year yield fell 2 basis points to 4.137% and the 10-year rose 1 basis point to 4.485%.

"Overall, this morning's data makes it difficult to envision a path toward a July Fed hike," said Ian Lyngen of Strategas. UOB struck a longer horizon: "We expect an extended period of policy pause through 2026 before the Fed resumes easing in 2027."

### US declines to renew USMCA as EU trade deal takes effect July 1

US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer declined to renew the USMCA during the pact's six-year joint review, held virtually on July 1. "The United States did not agree to renew the USMCA in its current form. As a result, the USMCA is not renewed," Greer said, citing the agreement's shortcomings and US trade deficits with Canada and Mexico. The treaty does not expire — it moves into a decade of rolling annual reviews under Article 34.7.4, leaving North American trade rules under recurring uncertainty. Canada and Mexico had sought a 16-year extension to 2042; Washington instead scheduled bilateral talks with Mexico for the week of July 20.

The same day, the US-EU trade deal took effect. Under the Turnberry agreement, a 15% tariff applies to most EU exports while duties on US industrial goods were removed, and the EU committed to buy $750 billion in American energy. Overlaying the bilateral deals is a Section 122 global surcharge of 10-15%, imposed February 24, which expires July 24 unless Congress extends it — the fallback Washington adopted after courts struck down the administration's earlier IEEPA-based tariff authority.

## 2. Politics & Diplomacy

### US-Iran talks pause as Khamenei's funeral draws millions to Tehran

Ayatollah Ali Khamenei's casket arrived at Tehran's Grand Mosalla on Friday, July 3, opening a national mourning period expected to draw 15-20 million people across five cities before his burial in Mashhad on July 9. Khamenei was killed in an airstrike in the opening hours of the US-Israeli war on Iran on February 28. General Ahmad Vahidi, head of Iran's Revolutionary Guard, attended the ceremony.

US-Iran negotiations paused for the funeral. Qatari and Pakistani mediators said talks would resume at the earliest possible time. The diplomacy runs on a 60-day window that opened June 17 with the Islamabad Memorandum, alongside a ceasefire signed the same day that declared a "permanent termination of military operations on all fronts." The talks center on administration of the Strait of Hormuz and the unfreezing of Iranian assets. "The denuclearization of Iran is moving along well," President Donald Trump said Wednesday.

### Iran threatens forceful response to tankers off designated Hormuz routes

Iran's joint military command, Khatam al-Anbiya, warned oil tankers on Thursday, July 3, to follow approved routes through the Strait of Hormuz or face attack. "Any failure to comply, deviation from the designated route, or disregard for navigation protocols of the Islamic Republic of Iran in the Strait of Hormuz will be met with an immediate and forceful response from the armed forces," the command said, adding that US interference would draw a rapid and decisive reaction.

The warning sharpens a dispute over Iran's bid to control passage through the waterway and levy transit fees — a plan Tehran floated jointly with Oman and one that Washington and Gulf states oppose. A ceasefire-era interim agreement grants 60 days of toll-free passage; Iranian analyses have pegged possible per-vessel charges as high as $1.5-2 million. Traffic is recovering unevenly: 258 vessels transited the prior week, up from 138, but still below the roughly 130 daily crossings before the war. Iranian forces attacked deviating vessels on June 25 and 27.

### Supreme Court term expands Trump's power but blocks birthright and tariff moves

The Supreme Court closed its 2025-2026 term with a series of rulings that broadened executive authority while checking the White House on immigration and trade. In Trump v. Slaughter, a 6-3 majority held that Trump lawfully fired FTC Commissioner Rebecca Kelly Slaughter, overturning the 91-year-old Humphrey's Executor precedent that had shielded officials at independent multimember agencies from at-will removal. In NRSC v. FEC, the court loosened limits on coordinated party campaign expenditures.

The administration lost on other fronts. On June 30, a 6-3 ruling struck down Trump's executive order ending birthright citizenship as incompatible with the 14th Amendment, prompting Trump to urge Congress on Truth Social to legislate an end to it. The court had already invalidated the administration's IEEPA tariff authority on February 20 in Learning Resources v. Trump, forcing the pivot to Section 122. On June 25, the court ruled courts cannot review DHS decisions to terminate Temporary Protected Status, clearing removal of protections for about 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians effective July 27. Justice Elena Kagan dissented: "The statements fairly shout, in their racial undertones and overtones alike, that race entered into the president's resolve to remove Haitians from this country."

## 3. Technology & Industry

### Microsoft launches $2.5 billion Frontier Company to embed AI engineers in clients

Microsoft launched Microsoft Frontier Company on July 2, committing $2.5 billion and 6,000 industry and engineering experts to embed teams inside customers and build AI systems on-site. The unit, overseen by CEO of Commercial Business Judson Althoff, signed early partners including London Stock Exchange Group, Unilever, Land O'Lakes and Accenture. The model — putting engineers directly inside client operations rather than selling software over the counter — was pioneered by Palantir and has become the industry's hot approach.

Microsoft is the latest and largest entrant in a wave of such ventures. Amazon committed $1 billion to its own forward-deployed engineering organization just two days earlier, and OpenAI and Anthropic launched rival efforts in May. "This goes beyond what has been labeled as Forward-Deployed Engineering," Althoff said. "It will be the largest, most capable, outcome-driven engineering organization in the industry."

### Google and Amazon emissions climb 25% and 16% as AI buildout accelerates

Google's total carbon emissions rose 25% year over year and Amazon's climbed 16%, undercutting both companies' carbon-neutrality pledges as they race to build out AI capacity. Amazon alone added more than 1.2 gigawatts of new data-center capacity in the fourth quarter of 2025, and both firms have invested in natural gas to power the expansion. Most of the growth falls in indirect Scope 3 emissions — GPU purchases, construction materials and semiconductor manufacturing — the categories hardest for a company to control.

The scale of the buildout underlines the pressure. Roughly 3,000 new data centers are under construction across the US, adding to about 4,000 already operating. "These challenges aren't insurmountable, but tech companies must significantly increase renewable investments," the report warned.

### DHS confirms breach of intelligence-sharing network used for World Cup security

The Department of Homeland Security confirmed on July 2 that hackers breached its Homeland Security Information Network (HSIN), the platform federal, state and local governments and law enforcement use to share intelligence. The intrusion occurred between late May and early June and also touched a SharePoint system used for interagency collaboration. "DHS immediately took action to isolate the affected systems," the department said, adding that classified networks were not affected. What data was stolen, and by whom, remains unknown.

The breach carries added weight because HSIN is being used to coordinate security for the 2026 FIFA World Cup, now underway across US host cities. Senator Mark Warner (D-VA), ranking member of the Senate Intelligence Committee, warned that although the shared intelligence is unclassified, "the information is highly sensitive, and its exposure risks national security."

### Record heat dome bakes half the US as July 4th temperature records fall

A heat dome parked over half the US is on track to break more than 300 temperature records by Saturday, July 4, with readings running 20-30 degrees above normal and up to 90 million Americans affected. New York City tied its daily record at 100 degrees on Thursday, matching a mark set in 1966; Boston hit 100 degrees for the 29th time on record, a new daily high; and Washington, D.C. was forecast to reach 103 on Friday and 101 on Independence Day, against a July 4 record of 100 set in 1919.

The heat is reshaping holiday plans. Philadelphia canceled its July 4 Salute to Independence parade, and the city's Liberty Medal ceremony moved indoors. Heat is the leading cause of weather-related deaths in the US.

### Colorado wildfires scorch 129,000 acres as Aspen Acres Fire burns uncontained

Six large wildfires have burned more than 129,000 acres — roughly 200 square miles — across Colorado, with fire officials warning of critical conditions through the July 4 weekend. The Aspen Acres Fire, 10 miles northwest of Rye in Pueblo and Custer counties, has consumed 50,187 acres at 0% containment and destroyed at least 180 structures, forcing evacuations in Wetmore, Beulah, Rye and San Isabel. "I've been a firefighter in Colorado for 40 years and I have never seen conditions like we're seeing out there right now," said Fire Division Director Mike Morgan, describing humidity below 5%, triple-digit temperatures and winds of 80-100 mph.

The response has pulled in crews from across the country, including a 650-person Alaska crew and 100 engines from California. Three federal firefighters were killed earlier on the Snyder Fire, which has burned 30,193 acres and reached 49% containment. Two other fires — Gold Mountain at 18,005 acres and Ferris at 27,382 acres — remain at 0% containment.

### Student loan overhaul takes effect July 1 as OBBBA ends the SAVE plan

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act's student loan provisions took effect July 1, terminating the SAVE income-driven repayment plan for new borrowers and sending 90-day notices to about 7 million current enrollees; SAVE fully sunsets in July 2028. Borrowers taking loans on or after July 1 face a narrowed menu: a Tiered Standard Plan running 10-25 years and a new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) that sets payments as a share of income.

The law also imposes borrowing caps — Parent PLUS loans are limited to $20,000 a year and $65,000 total, and graduate students to $20,500 a year with a $100,000 lifetime cap. Elsewhere, OBBBA expanded Pell Grants to short-term workforce training and eliminated the EV charging equipment tax credit, which expired June 30. The law's "Trump accounts" begin July 4.

### US men beat Bosnia 2-0 for first World Cup knockout win since 2002

The US men's national team beat Bosnia and Herzegovina 2-0 on July 1 at Levi's Stadium in Santa Clara, California, sealing its first World Cup knockout win since 2002 — only the second in program history. Folarin Balogun scored just before halftime, and Malik Tillman added a free kick in the 82nd minute after the US had been reduced to 10 players by Balogun's late red card, which suspends him for the Round of 16 against Belgium on July 6.

The result carries stakes beyond the pitch. The 2026 tournament, hosted across the US, Canada and Mexico, is projected by FIFA to add $17 billion to US GDP and 185,000 jobs.
