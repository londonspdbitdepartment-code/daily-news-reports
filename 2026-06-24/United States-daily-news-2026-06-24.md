### Trump orders DOJ probe into oil companies over pump prices as crude slides

President Donald Trump posted on Truth Social shortly after midnight on June 24, 2026, instructing the Department of Justice to immediately investigate major oil companies for failing to pass falling crude costs through to drivers. "The big Oil Companies are not dropping their price at the pump commensurate with the sharply lower prices they are paying for Oil," Trump wrote.

The arithmetic behind the complaint: crude oil has fallen roughly 27% over the past month, from $104 per barrel to just under $76, after an interim deal reopened the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil supply flows. The chokepoint had been at the center of the Iran war earlier in 2026; Iran declared the strait closed in March, and a memorandum of understanding signed in mid-June committed Tehran to reopening transit, sending crude prices sharply lower. Pump prices have not kept pace — gasoline at the pump declined only about 13% over the same stretch, leaving the national average for regular gas at $3.93 per gallon, below $4 for the first time since March 30, 2026.

The lag Trump is targeting is well-documented and largely mechanical. Studies of crude-to-pump pass-through show roughly 13% of a crude move reaches the pump after one week, 37% after three months, and about 50% over the long term — refining margins, inventory turnover, and retail contracts all delay the transmission. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent separately stressed the need to diversify away from dangerous concentrations of supply. No oil companies were named, and the DOJ had announced no details of any inquiry as of June 24 morning.

### Federal Reserve to release 2026 stress-test results for 32 large banks

The Federal Reserve Board will release results of its 2026 annual bank stress test on Wednesday, June 24, at 4:00 p.m. EDT, the regulator said. The test covers 32 large U.S. banks, including JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, and Morgan Stanley — the roster expanded to 32 institutions this year, up from 22 in 2025.

The 2026 severely adverse scenario models a sharp recession: unemployment rising nearly 5.5 percentage points to a peak of 10%, a 30% decline in house prices, and a 39% decline in commercial real estate values, alongside corporate-debt stress. The results determine each bank's stress capital buffer (SCB), the cushion of capital it must hold above minimum requirements before facing automatic limits on dividends and share buybacks. The buffer is set as the peak-to-trough fall in a bank's CET1 ratio plus a quarter of planned dividends, with a 2.5% floor.

The stakes for capital returns are softened this year by an earlier procedural move. The Fed voted in February 2026 to maintain current stress capital buffer requirements unchanged through 2027, holding existing buffers in place while the Board works on a separate proposal to average results across two years and reduce the swings that have frustrated banks. In the 2025 test, all 22 large banks were found well-positioned, with enough capital to absorb nearly $550 billion in hypothetical losses while continuing to lend.

## 2. Politics & Diplomacy

### IAEA's Grossi says Iran inspections will proceed despite Tehran's denial

IAEA Director General Rafael Mariano Grossi confirmed on June 24, 2026 that inspections of Iranian nuclear sites will proceed, directly contradicting Iran's foreign ministry, which had denied any agreement allowing inspector access to disputed sites was in place. Grossi's June 24 statement reasserted the IAEA's authority to verify Iran's program.

The dispute sits at the heart of post-war diplomacy. After U.S. and Israeli strikes hit Iran's enrichment sites at Fordow, Natanz, and Esfahan earlier in 2026, the IAEA halted its verification activities and lost access to several of Iran's most sensitive facilities. Grossi has said the agency cannot determine with certainty the degree of damage inside Fordow's underground enrichment halls, though very significant damage to centrifuge machinery is expected. Re-establishing inspector access is the central test of whether the mid-June ceasefire holds, because without it the world has no independent account of where Iran's stockpile of enriched uranium now stands.

Tehran's flat denial that any access deal exists exposes the gap between the negotiated framework and what Iran is prepared to concede on the ground. The interim accord deferred Iran's enrichment rights to later-stage talks, leaving the inspection question as the immediate flashpoint.

### Trump works the Senate at GOP lunch to push the SAVE Act

President Trump traveled to Capitol Hill on June 24, 2026 for a Republican Senate lunch focused on the SAVE Act — legislation that would require documentary proof of citizenship to register to vote. With the margin exceedingly narrow, Trump pressed GOP senators who have voiced reservations, while the bill had already passed the House in an earlier session.

The personal lobbying reflects how stubborn the math has been. The measure stalled in the Senate once already in 2026, falling short of the 60 votes needed to clear a filibuster; some Republicans floated curtailing the filibuster to muscle it through, but Majority Leader John Thune found no broad appetite for that within the conference. Under the bill, most voters would have to present a passport or certified birth certificate in person, effectively ending most online and mail registration.

The policy rationale remains contested. Election officials and experts note that noncitizen voting is already illegal and has been documented only at microscopic levels, and critics warn the in-person documentary requirement could burden eligible citizens — including married women whose current name does not match their birth certificate.

Slate has accumulated more than 160,000 refundable reservations, closed a $650 million Series C that lifted total funding to about $1.4 billion, and earmarked nearly $400 million for a factory in Warsaw, Indiana, on a former printing-plant site, projected to create more than 2,000 jobs. First deliveries are targeted for late 2026.

## 4. Society & Livelihood

### Appeals court revives Trump's expanded fast-track deportations

A federal appeals court on June 24, 2026 upheld the Trump administration's expanded use of expedited removal, reversing a lower-court block. Expedited removal allows immigration authorities to deport people quickly without a full immigration-court hearing; the administration extended it well beyond the border zone to undocumented immigrants anywhere in the country who cannot prove at least two years of continuous residence.

The case turns on due process in the interior. Under the prior policy, fast-track removal applied only near the border to recent entrants who could not show two weeks of presence. After the administration's 2025 expansion, a Biden-appointed district judge found the policy likely trampled due-process rights and stayed it; the appeals court disagreed and threw out that block, clearing the way for nationwide use to resume.

Civil-liberties advocates warned the ruling could push people with meritorious asylum claims out of the country before they get a hearing, since the expedited process compresses the window in which a migrant can raise a fear-of-persecution claim.

### France confirms Europe's first Ebola case in the outbreak as CDC tightens US entry

France confirmed its first Ebola case on June 24, 2026 — a healthcare worker who returned from a humanitarian mission in the Democratic Republic of Congo — marking the first case on European soil in the current outbreak. The patient is being treated under strict biosafety protocols, contact tracing is under way, and close contacts must self-isolate for 21 days, the disease's full incubation window.

The strain raises the stakes. The current outbreak is caused by Bundibugyo virus, against which there is no licensed vaccine or specific treatment; the two approved Ebola vaccines, Ervebo and the Mvabea/Zabdeno regimen, target the Zaire species, and the WHO has recommended against deploying them here on insufficient evidence. The DRC outbreak, centered in Ituri province since May 2026, has killed more than 260 people and infected more than 1,000, with a case-fatality rate that past Bundibugyo episodes put at 30% to 50%.

The U.S. response is already in motion. The US CDC issued a Title 42 order on June 21, 2026 suspending the introduction of specified foreign nationals from Ebola-affected countries, with screening at JFK, Atlanta, and Dulles; U.S. citizens are still admitted under enhanced screening. The CDC has assessed the immediate risk to the general U.S. public as low.

### Depo-Provera meningioma litigation reaches make-or-break Daubert hearing

General causation Daubert hearings in MDL-3140 (Depo-Provera meningioma litigation) are scheduled to commence on June 24, 2026 before U.S. District Judge M. Casey Rodgers in the Northern District of Florida, running June 24-26. The hearings will decide whether plaintiffs' experts may testify that Depo-Provera causes meningioma — a gatekeeping ruling that effectively determines whether the litigation survives.

The numbers underscore the stakes. As of June 2026, the MDL has 5,508 cases pending, and the scientific record at issue is substantial: a BMJ study found a 5.55x odds ratio linking the contraceptive to meningioma, while a JAMA study found a 2.43x risk. The FDA added a meningioma warning to Depo-Provera in December 2025, a regulatory marker plaintiffs are expected to lean on.

If the experts clear the Daubert bar, the case proceeds toward trial: bellwether trials (Blonski v. Pfizer et al.) are scheduled to begin December 7, 2026, with Pfizer as the primary defendant.
