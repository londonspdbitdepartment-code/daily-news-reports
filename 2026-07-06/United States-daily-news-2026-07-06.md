### Treasury yields ease as Wall Street awaits June FOMC minutes, Dow closes at record 52,900

The benchmark 10-year Treasury yield fell nearly 2 basis points to 4.459% at 4:26 a.m. ET on Monday, July 6, 2026, as traders returned from the Independence Day break to a thin economic calendar and fixed their attention on the June 16–17 FOMC meeting minutes, due Wednesday, July 8. The 2-year note slipped more than 1 basis point to 4.112% and the 30-year bond shed 1 basis point to 4.969%.

The minutes carry unusual weight because June was Kevin Warsh's first meeting as Fed chair. The committee held the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% for a fourth consecutive meeting, but the dot plot turned hawkish: the median year-end 2026 projection moved up to 3.8% from 3.4% in March, erasing an earlier signal for a cut as policymakers weighed an inflation spike tied to the Iran war. Warsh himself declined to submit a projection, telling reporters the tool is "not helpful in the conduct of policy." "We recognize that inflation has been running well ahead of the Fed's long-stated inflation goal of 2 percent — that's been going on for more than five years. Persistently high prices are a burden for the American people," Warsh said.

Equities split. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 1.14% (594.83 points) to a record close of 52,900.07, while the Nasdaq Composite slid 0.8% (207.36 points) to 25,832.67 as AI semiconductor names sold off — Micron (MU) fell 5.5%, AMD dropped 4.3% and Intel (INTC) lost 5.3%. The S&P 500 closed at 7,483.24.

### USTR forced-labor tariff comment window closes as hearings on 10%–12.5% duties covering 60 economies begin July 7

July 6, 2026 marked the deadline for public written comments in the US Trade Representative's Section 301 forced-labor investigation covering 60 trading partners, and public hearings begin Tuesday, July 7 at the US International Trade Commission at 10:00 a.m. ET. USTR found that 54 of the 60 economies fail to enforce a prohibition on imports produced with forced labor, and proposed a 10% additional tariff on goods from economies with some form of forced-labor import ban and 12.5% on the rest — spanning 99.4% of US trading partners under Federal Register dockets USTR-2026-0265 and USTR-2026-0266. USTR also held hearings July 6 on a parallel Brazil Section 301 investigation.

The timing is not incidental. The 10% Section 122 global surcharge — itself a fallback erected after the Supreme Court struck down Trump's IEEPA-based tariffs earlier in 2026 — expires July 24, 2026, after the US Court of International Trade invalidated it in May. The new Section 301 duties are engineered to slide into that gap, providing legal continuity for the tariff wall. USTR estimates the measures would raise nearly $1 trillion over 10 years.

### June payrolls rise just 57,000 as participation hits five-year low, cementing Fed's hold

Nonfarm payrolls rose only 57,000 in June, the BLS reported July 2, 2026 — roughly half the 115,000 consensus and down from a downwardly revised 129,000 in May. Unemployment ticked down to 4.2% from 4.3%, but for the wrong reason: labor-force participation fell 0.3 percentage point to 61.5%, the lowest since March 2021, meaning the jobless rate improved partly because people stopped looking for work. Hiring clustered in professional and business services, social assistance and healthcare, while leisure and hospitality shed jobs.

By Monday, July 6 the market's base case had hardened: Fed Chair Warsh holds the funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% through at least September 2026, caught between a softening labor market and inflation still running above target. Annual wage growth held at 3.9% year over year, offering the Fed little room to declare victory on either mandate.

### Marriage-based green cards face new scrutiny, splitting some couples from their US-citizen spouses

USCIS issued internal guidance in early 2026 directing heightened scrutiny to all family-based petitions, and NPR reported July 6, 2026 that the crackdown now reaches couples once treated as routine. Mandatory in-person interviews have become standard, and "Stokes interviews" — in which spouses are separated and questioned individually, then their answers compared — are increasingly common; the practice, named for a 1975 federal court decision, was historically reserved for cases with strong fraud indicators. Officers now demand evidence of "good moral character" not previously required and weigh whether applicants returned to their home country to file.

The tightening sits inside a broader enforcement push. The administration paused immigrant visas for people from 75 countries, and the Supreme Court's June 25, 2026 ruling (6-3) for the administration stripped protections from 350,000 Haitian and Syrian TPS holders. Some non-US-citizen spouses have been physically separated from their US-citizen partners and now fear engaging with the system at all.

### First intergovernmental AI summit opens in Geneva as scientists warn control is "not guaranteed"

The first session of the UN Global Dialogue on AI Governance convened July 6–7, 2026 at Geneva's Palexpo, gathering 193 UN member states alongside the private sector, civil society and academia — the first dedicated intergovernmental forum on AI governance at the UN level, co-chaired by Ambassador Egriselda López of El Salvador and Ambassador Rein Tammsaar of Estonia. It runs concurrently with the WSIS Forum 2026 (July 6–10) and the ITU AI for Good Global Summit (July 7–10).

An independent scientific panel of 40 experts, co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio and Nobel laureate Maria Ressa, delivered the summit's starkest message. "Science currently cannot guarantee that as capabilities continue to increase, AI will not cause catastrophic harm," Bengio said, the panel finding no technical guarantee that the most advanced systems will reliably follow human instructions. The forum surfaced the governance problem's geometry: Tammsaar noted that "frontier developers are basically concentrated in two countries — the US and China," and the US alone controls roughly 75% of the computing power behind the world's 500 top AI supercomputers. That concentration collides with Washington's posture — the White House OSTP has previously rejected "centralized control and global governance" of AI.

### One year after OBBBA, 1.2 million fewer ACA enrollees and Medicaid budgets set to shrink $679 billion

One year after Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act into law on July 4, 2025, the Center for American Progress used the anniversary week to tally the fallout. ACA marketplace plan selections fell 1.2 million (−5%) for the 2026 enrollment period versus 2025 — the largest single-year decline since the exchanges opened in 2014 — as enhanced premium tax credits expired. Average monthly net premiums rose 58%, from $113 in 2025 to $178 in 2026, and average deductibles climbed 37% to a record $3,786.

The food-aid picture is similar: SNAP participation dropped 10%, more than 4 million people, between July 2025 and March 2026, with children on SNAP falling by over 700,000 across 12 states with available data. Looking forward, RAND projects the law's Medicaid provisions will cut states' Medicaid budgets by nearly $679 billion cumulatively and could cause 7.6 million coverage losses by 2034, while CBO projects the health provisions leave 10 million more people uninsured by that year. DHS marked the same anniversary a "historic achievement."

### PECO strike enters third day as storm outages compound Philadelphia power crisis

By the morning of July 6, 2026 — the third day of the first labor stoppage in PECO's 145-year history — about 1,600 members of IBEW Local 614 remained on strike against the Philadelphia-area electric and gas utility, which is owned by Exelon. The walkout began at 12:01 a.m. Saturday, July 4, after negotiations collapsed despite more than 12 hours of talks. The timing could hardly be worse: Saturday night storms knocked out power to about 60,000 customers, and by Sunday evening around 5,000 were still dark, with some restoration potentially slipping to Tuesday. PECO brought in crews from North Carolina, South Carolina and Florida to cover.

PECO says its offer amounts to a cumulative 20% wage increase over five years plus enhanced retirement and medical benefits; the union counters that its members earn about 30% less than counterparts at comparable utilities. "We will not accept a contract that undercuts the women of our union," an IBEW Local 614 representative said.

### Super Typhoon Bavi makes landfall on US territories of Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands

Super Typhoon Bavi, a Category 5-equivalent storm packing maximum sustained winds of 180 mph (290 km/h), scored a direct hit on Rota — an island of about 2,000 people in the US Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands — just after 9:00 a.m. local time Monday, July 6, 2026. By 2:00 p.m. local the center sat about 60 miles west of Rota, 75 miles west of Guam and 100 miles southwest of Tinian and Saipan, tracking west-northwest at about 9 mph toward the Philippines. Guam saw sustained winds of 40–80 mph with gusts of 90–100 mph.

Rota authorities reported "major damages" amid communication outages, and across the Marianas and Guam fallen trees, downed lines, rockslides and flooding rendered roughly 10 major roads impassable, with widespread power and water outages. The National Weather Service called conditions "catastrophic" and "life-threatening," urging residents to "treat these imminent extreme winds as if a tornado was approaching." Three US military installations — Andersen Air Force Base, Marine Corps Base Camp Blaz and Naval Base Guam — were placed at top readiness. No casualties were formally reported by early evening. The storm followed Super Typhoon Sinlaku, which struck the region in April 2026 and caused over $450 million in damage to Guam; the combined population of Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands is about 210,000.

### China frees underground church pastor Ezra Jin after Trump's personal appeal to Xi Jinping

Pastor Ezra Jin Mingri, 57, founder and senior pastor of Beijing's Zion Church — one of China's largest unregistered Protestant congregations — arrived in Los Angeles on July 4, 2026 after being released directly from Chinese detention, the Christian human-rights group ChinaAid confirmed July 6, 2026. Jin had been held since October 10, 2025 on suspicion of "illegal use of information networks," part of a coordinated sweep that detained 18 church leaders in what rights groups called the largest crackdown on an urban house church in 40 years.

His release came less than two months after Trump personally raised Jin's case during a state visit to Beijing in May 2026, where Xi Jinping said he would "strongly consider" freeing him. ChinaAid said Chinese officials told Jin his release "resulted from discussions between US President Donald J. Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping." "We thank God for this tremendous miracle," said Jin's daughter Grace. Zion Church, shut by authorities in 2018 after refusing to install facial-recognition surveillance, kept an online presence during the pandemic reaching followers across 40 Chinese cities. Human Rights Watch noted at least eight other Zion members detained in October 2025 remain imprisoned.
