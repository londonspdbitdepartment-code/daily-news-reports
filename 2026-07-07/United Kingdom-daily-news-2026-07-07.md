### Bank of England flags AI as a "growing risk" to stability, moves to ease bank capital rules

The Bank of England named artificial intelligence a "growing risk" to financial stability in its Financial Stability Report, released 7 July 2026, and set out a linked plan for the Prudential Regulation Authority to ease capital requirements on UK lenders. It is the first Report to elevate AI to a headline systemic concern.

The Bank's worry is concrete. Frontier AI models are now capable enough to exploit software vulnerabilities, raising the sophistication and impact of cyber attacks on banks and market infrastructure. It flagged concentration risk from the industry's reliance on a small number of cloud and model providers, and warned that AI-driven, increasingly agentic trading systems could amplify shocks and move markets in correlated ways no single desk controls. Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden urged bespoke regulation tailored to AI rather than bolting the technology onto existing rules.

Running alongside the risk warning is a loosening. The PRA plans to reduce leverage requirements on large domestic-focused UK banks by around 20 basis points under a reworked capital-buffer framework — a partial unwind of post-2007-crisis rules — with the exact figure varying by bank and a consultation expected to conclude next year. The Bank stressed UK lenders and households remain resilient. UK bank stocks ticked up in morning trading on 7 July.

### OBR warns UK debt would grow "explosively" without corrective action

The Office for Budget Responsibility said UK public debt would grow "explosively" absent policy change, in its Fiscal Risks and Sustainability (FRS) report published 7 July 2026 — the fiscal watchdog's first full update of its long-run projections since 2023.

Almost every 50-year scenario the OBR modelled showed debt rising on an unsustainable path. The pressures compound: an ageing population driving NHS and social-care costs, a rising debt-interest bill, the fiscal weight of the climate transition, and higher defence spending under NATO's 3% of GDP target. The watchdog framed its numbers as a warning rather than a forecast — future governments are expected to act before debt reaches those levels — but cautioned that the longer correction is delayed, the larger the tax rises or spending cuts required, and the heavier the burden shifted onto future generations.

The report lands squarely on Chancellor Rachel Reeves, who faces an October 2026 Budget with limited fiscal headroom and a debt trajectory the OBR says cannot be left to drift.

### Halifax house prices rise 0.2% in June, first monthly gain in four months

UK average house prices rose 0.2% month-on-month in June, the first monthly gain after three months of decline, according to the Halifax House Price Index published 7 July 2026. Annual growth held at roughly 2.1%.

The uptick came against a squeezed household backdrop: the energy price cap rose 13% from 1 July, lifting bills for millions just as buyers weigh affordability. The lender attributed the modest recovery to steadier mortgage rates and resilient demand at the lower end of the market. The June reading is the last to carry the Halifax name — from July 2026 the series is rebranded the Lloyds House Price Index, with the methodology unchanged.

### Starmer heads to Ankara NATO summit, likely his last before Labour handover to Burnham

Prime Minister Keir Starmer travelled to Ankara on 7 July 2026 for what is expected to be his final NATO summit before handing over the Labour leadership to Andy Burnham. The summit is the second NATO has held in Turkey, after Istanbul in 2004.

The timing is stark. Starmer announced on 22 June that he would resign as Labour leader and Prime Minister after losing the confidence of his MPs; Burnham, who won the Makerfield by-election in June to secure a Commons seat, is the presumptive successor. Labour leadership nominations open on 9 July, and if Burnham stands unopposed he could be confirmed within days. Starmer arrives in Ankara with less than a fortnight likely left in Downing Street.

The agenda is collective defence, the NATO 3% of GDP spending target, and conflicts on the alliance's eastern flank. The Conservative opposition has pressed on whether a leadership transition mid-summit threatens continuity in the UK's NATO commitments and its support for arms to Ukraine.

### Railways Bill clears Lords Second Reading, moving Great British Railways closer to law

The Railways Bill received its Second Reading in the House of Lords on 7 July 2026, advancing Labour's flagship rail-renationalisation programme. The Bill establishes Great British Railways (GBR) as a publicly owned body to manage rail infrastructure and integrate operations across England, uniting track and train under a single "directing mind".

The Bill has already passed the Commons and delivers on Labour's 2024 manifesto pledge to bring passenger services into public ownership as private franchise contracts expire — avoiding compensation payouts to operators. That transfer is already under way: South Western Railway moved into public hands in May 2025, followed by c2c, Greater Anglia, West Midlands Trains and Govia Thameslink Railway through to May 2026. GBR would provide the permanent structure to hold them. Incoming PM Andy Burnham, a former Transport Secretary, inherits the programme at the point it becomes statutory.

### Commons considers Lords amendments to National Security (State Threats) Bill

The House of Commons considered Lords amendments to the National Security (State Threats) Bill on 7 July 2026, bringing legislation to counter hostile state activity within reach of Royal Assent. The Bill strengthens powers against foreign interference, espionage, and sabotage of critical infrastructure, and creates a category of designated organisations linked to foreign state threats that it becomes an offence to support or benefit from.

The Lords returned the Bill with amendments on judicial oversight, tighter "foreign principal" definitions, and whistleblower protections, alongside a defence carved out for those carrying out humanitarian activities. The Bill has drawn broad cross-party support, though some peers and civil-liberties groups have pressed on proportionality — whether the designation powers reach too widely. Clearing the Commons stage leaves the Bill close to becoming law.

## 3. Technology & Industry

### More than 60 UK businesses sign government's Cyber Resilience Pledge

More than 60 businesses signed the UK government's Cyber Resilience Pledge at its launch on 7 July 2026, a voluntary commitment to baseline defences hosted through the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT). Founding signatories span financial services, critical infrastructure, retail and technology, including M&S, Nationwide, ITV, Microsoft UK and Cloudflare.

Science and Technology Secretary Liz Kendall announced the initiative, which asks firms to adopt practical measures — multi-factor authentication, staff training, patching and incident-response planning — and to treat cyber risk as a board-level responsibility. The pledge is a voluntary bridge ahead of the Cyber Security and Resilience Act, the most significant overhaul of UK cyber regulation since the 2018 NIS framework, currently moving through Parliament. Ministers used the launch to press firms to strengthen supply-chain controls and speed up incident reporting.

### Justice Committee hears evidence on Immigration and Asylum Bill appeals overhaul

The Commons Justice Committee took oral evidence on 7 July 2026 (14:30, Grimond Room, Portcullis House) on the appeals provisions of the Immigration and Asylum Bill, questioning legal experts over a plan to strip immigration appeals out of the Upper Tribunal. Witnesses were Nadine Finch, a former Upper Tribunal judge; Professor Joe Tomlinson, Professor of Administrative Law at King's College London; and Alison Stanley, Senior Consultant at Bindmans LLP, appearing for The Law Society.

The Bill, introduced 30 June 2026, would abolish Upper Tribunal-based appeals and replace them with a new Independent Immigration Appeals Authority (IIAA). The Law Society and Bar Council have raised concerns about the new body's independence from government. The scrutiny lands against a hard backdrop: in the year to March 2026 the UK recorded 44,000 detected illegal entries, 39,000 of them by small boat. The Home Secretary appeared the same day before the Lords Justice and Home Affairs Committee. The Bill's Commons Second Reading is scheduled for 13 July 2026.

DCC plc faces mounting shareholder opposition to a contested £5.7bn takeover by KKR and ECP (Energy Capital Partners) as a key vote deadline of 8 July 2026 approaches, the Irish Times reported on 7 July 2026. Major investors argue the offer undervalues the company.

## 6. Other Notable Events

### London marks 21st anniversary of 7/7 bombings with Hyde Park memorial

The 21st anniversary memorial service for the 7 July 2005 London bombings was held on 7 July 2026 at Hyde Park, with a livestream beginning at 11:50 and a minute's silence at 11:58. The 2005 attacks killed 52 people and injured over 700 in coordinated suicide bombings on the London Underground and a bus.

The service was organised by the Greater London Authority with survivors' groups and the 7/7 Memorial Trust. The Mayor of London and representatives of the emergency services laid wreaths at the permanent memorial — 52 stainless-steel columns on the east side of Hyde Park, grouped to reflect the four attack sites at Aldgate, Edgware Road, King's Cross and Tavistock Square. This year's commemoration falls as the National Security (State Threats) Bill, which strengthens the UK's powers against hostile threats, moves through its final parliamentary stages.
