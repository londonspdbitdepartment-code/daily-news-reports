### UK manufacturing PMI holds at 52.5 in June as export orders slip on US tariff strain

S&P Global's UK Manufacturing PMI came in at 52.5 for June 2026, holding above the 50-point line that separates expansion from contraction for a second consecutive month and beating forecasts of a sharper slowdown. The reading, released 1 July 2026, cooled only marginally from May's 52.8 print.

The detail underneath the headline is where the strain shows. New export orders slipped as buyers hesitated amid uncertainty over US tariff policy, while domestic orders held firm — propped up in part by pre-tariff stockpiling as customers pulled purchases forward ahead of expected duties. The output sub-index climbed to a four-month high, suggesting factories are still clearing work even as forward-looking demand softens.

Manufacturers pointed to currency swings, energy costs, and supply-chain repricing as the main pressures on margins. The stabilisation matters because UK factory activity spent the early part of the year in contraction; two months above 50 mark a tentative recovery. But the export weakness leaves a clear downside risk into the third quarter, when the pre-tariff order cushion runs out and US trade measures bite harder.

### UK steel trade measure replaces safeguard from 1 July, cutting tariff-free quota 60%

The UK's new Steel Trade Measure took effect 1 July 2026, replacing the Steel Safeguard Measure that Britain had carried over from its EU membership and that was due to expire the same day. It cuts overall tariff-free import quota volumes by 60% against the old safeguard and slaps a 50% additional tariff on any imports above the threshold, across 15 product categories.

The Department for Business and Trade designed the tighter regime to shield domestic producers from an import surge as the old 25% safeguard duty lapsed. The backdrop is stark: UK crude steel production has more than halved over the past decade, hit by a wave of cheap imports tied to global overcapacity that is driven largely by Chinese output. The move brings Britain into line with defensive action already taken by the US, EU, and Canada.

Quota access runs on a first-come, first-served basis administered by HMRC and is allocated quarterly through to 30 June 2027. Trade bodies have warned the tighter quotas could raise input costs and complicate logistics for smaller importers, who now face the 50% wall far sooner in the year than under the previous framework.

### UK annual house price growth edges up to 2.2% in June as regions all turn positive

UK annual house price growth accelerated to 2.2% in June 2026, up from 1.7% in May, according to the Nationwide House Price Index released 1 July 2026. The average UK house price was £277,484, down marginally from £278,024 the month before — broadly flat month-on-month after seasonal adjustment, so the pickup is an annual-comparison effect rather than a fresh surge.

Every one of the UK's 13 regions recorded positive annual growth in the second quarter, the widest spread of gains in the report. Northern Ireland led at 8.6% year-on-year; Scotland and Wales each posted 3.5%. English growth accelerated to 1.5% from 0.9% in the first quarter, with the North West the strongest English region at 3.9%. The south stayed subdued — southern England at 0.7% and London at 1.6%.

Robert Gardner, Nationwide's chief economist, tied the recent softening to external shocks. "It is not surprising that the market has softened a little in recent months, given the uncertainty caused by developments in the Middle East and the subsequent rise in energy prices and market interest rates," he said. He flagged that consumer confidence and housing sentiment had weakened and that mortgage approvals fell in May.

### Commons passes Taxation (Energy and Vehicles) Bill, lifting Electricity Generator Levy to 55%

The House of Commons cleared all remaining stages of the Taxation (Energy and Vehicles) Bill in a single sitting on 1 July 2026, completing its passage just a week after the government introduced it on 24 June. The bill's centrepiece raises the Electricity Generator Levy from 45% to 55%, effective 1 July 2026.

The EGL is a windfall tax on generators' exceptional receipts above a benchmark price, introduced in January 2023 to capture the surplus revenues low-carbon generators earned during the energy-price spike. It was due to wind down by March 2028; the government has now both raised the rate and signalled it will extend the levy beyond that date, citing sustained energy-price rises driven by the Middle East conflict.

The bill carries two other measures approved via Ways and Means resolutions of 24 June: tax-free Approved Mileage Allowance Payments rise from 45p to 55p per mile, backdated to the start of the 2026-27 tax year, and a temporary 12-month £1 reduced Vehicle Excise Duty rate applies to specified HGVs for licences taken between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027. The same day, the Commons also passed the Supply and Appropriation (Main Estimates) Bill, authorising £1.15 trillion of departmental spending for 2026-27.

### Lords press for jobs and oversight safeguards on British Steel nationalisation bill

Peers tabled a raft of new safeguards as the House of Lords continued committee-stage scrutiny of the Steel Industry (Nationalisation) Bill on its second committee day, 1 July 2026. The amendments would require a stakeholder advisory committee of workers, communities, and industry, publication of a jobs-security and industrial-transition strategy report at least 90 days before any nationalisation order, and added compensation and oversight guarantees.

The bill hands the Secretary of State power to transfer a steel company's shares or property into public ownership on public-interest grounds. Its principal target is British Steel Limited, owned by China's Jingye Group. The government has said it is "strongly minded to nationalise" the company to protect jobs and preserve primary steelmaking at Scunthorpe. That threat has been building for over a year: Jingye moved in March 2025 to close the Scunthorpe blast furnaces over losses it put at £700,000 a day, prompting emergency intervention in April 2025 under which the Department for Business and Trade spent £377 million in nine months keeping the furnaces running.

The current bill moves fast. Introduced to the Commons on 14 May 2026, it was fast-tracked through all Commons stages by 9 June, cleared its Lords second reading on 16 June, and entered committee stage on 25 June — a compressed timetable that has left peers pressing for the accountability provisions the emergency route skipped.

## 3. Technology & Industry

### Ofcom adds self-harm and cyberflashing as priority offences under Online Safety Act

Ofcom published updated Illegal Harms Codes and guidance under the Online Safety Act 2023 on 1 July 2026, folding in two priority offences the government designated in December 2025: encouraging or assisting serious self-harm, and cyberflashing — the sending of unsolicited explicit images. The change expands the regulator's illegal-harm framework from 17 to 18 groups.

The two offences are handled differently in the codes. Serious self-harm is merged with suicide-related content into a single "suicide and self-harm" category that providers must risk assess, while cyberflashing becomes a new standalone category assessed on its own. The two now sit alongside the Act's list of over 130 priority offences — terrorism, illegal drugs and weapons, child sexual abuse — against which platforms must take proactive measures rather than merely react to reports.

For UK online service providers the practical burden is largely administrative: updating risk assessments, tracking and analysis systems, and moderation policies so the two categories are captured. The amended codes still need parliamentary approval and are expected to come into force in autumn 2026. Ofcom ran its consultation from March to April 2026 and incorporated inputs from Microsoft and advocacy organisations.

### Bank of England's Breeden warns autonomous AI could trigger market meltdowns, floats kill switches

Autonomous AI agents let loose in financial markets could amplify volatility enough to trigger market meltdowns, Bank of England Deputy Governor for Financial Stability Sarah Breeden warned at the ECB Forum on Central Banking in Sintra, Portugal, on 30 June 2026, arguing that existing supervisory frameworks are not built for the risk. Reuters carried the remarks on 1 July.

The Bank is weighing two novel tools. The first is market-wide "kill switches" — circuit breakers that would halt automated trading when faulty or misaligned AI models are detected causing systemic disruption. The second is "enhanced recovery" arrangements that would let one bank temporarily assume another's core functions after an AI failure. Breeden's central worry is correlated behaviour: if many firms run similarly-trained agents, they could act identically and simultaneously, turning a localised glitch into a system-wide cascade.

"Our frameworks were not built to contemplate autonomous agents, and relying on a human in the loop for all agent actions is unlikely to be realistic," Breeden said, signalling that supervisors cannot lean on manual override as a catch-all safeguard. She cited a Cambridge survey finding that 52% of financial firms already deploy agentic AI, underlining that this is a live exposure rather than a distant one. The Financial Stability Board in June 2026 had likewise called for safeguards.

## 4. Society & Livelihood

### UK Emissions Trading Scheme extends to maritime from 1 July, pricing domestic shipping carbon

Domestic shipping entered the UK Emissions Trading Scheme on 1 July 2026, bringing carbon pricing to the sector for the first time. The scheme now covers cargo and passenger vessels of 5,000 gross tonnage and above on UK-port-to-UK-port voyages, plus in-port emissions from vessels in both domestic and international trades. It captures not only CO2 but also methane (CH4) and nitrous oxide (N2O).

The UK approach differs sharply from the EU's. Brussels prices 100% of intra-EU voyages and 50% of international voyages touching an EU port; the UK scheme, by contrast, covers 100% of domestic UK-to-UK voyages only, capturing an estimated 2.5 million tonnes — roughly 15% of the sector's emissions — with international-voyage coverage not proposed until 2028. The two governments announced in May 2025 an intention to link their schemes, and the deferral of offshore ship inclusion to 1 January 2027 leaves the UK regime still building out.

Operators must submit an Emissions Monitoring Plan to a UKAS-accredited verifier and file Annual Emissions Reports by 31 March each year, with the first allowance surrender due 30 April 2028; compliance runs through the METS digital platform. The UK Chamber of Shipping raised "broad-reaching concerns" in February 2026 over competitiveness and the cost burden on island communities dependent on domestic ferry routes.

### UK launches Women in Research Charter as 60-plus institutions commit to family-leave floors

Over 60 research institutions signed the new Women in Research Charter at its launch on 1 July 2026, a voluntary framework unveiled by Science Secretary Liz Kendall MP to lift gender equity across the UK research sector. Signatories include Oxford, Cambridge, Edinburgh, King's College London, the Royal Society, the British Academy, and the Wellcome Trust.

The charter sets concrete floors rather than aspirations. Signatories commit to at least 52 weeks' maternity leave, with 26 weeks at full stipend; a minimum of two weeks' paid partner leave; and cover for pregnancy-related sickness, adoption, and neonatal care. They also pledge flexible part-time funding with adjusted assessment timelines, clear reporting channels for bullying, harassment and sexual misconduct, evaluation methods that accommodate career breaks and part-time work, and annual publication of grant success rates and staffing data broken down by sex.

"No woman should ever be held back from a fair shot at fulfilling their potential," Kendall said. The disaggregated-data requirement is the sharpest lever: by forcing institutions to publish grant success and staffing figures split by sex each year, the charter creates a running public record against which signatories can be measured.

International design professionals gained a dedicated route into the UK's Global Talent visa from 1 July 2026, with endorsement handled by Arts Council England. The pathway covers exceptional talent or exceptional promise in product, fashion, and graphic design, and design for digital technologies, broadening a visa that previously ran through Science, Engineering, Humanities, Medicine, Arts, Culture, and Digital Technology streams.

## 6. Other Notable Events

### UK Supreme Court backs HMRC in £200m BlueCrest tax case, tightening LLP salaried-member rules

The UK Supreme Court unanimously dismissed BlueCrest Capital Management (UK) LLP's appeal against HMRC on 1 July 2026, ruling that certain senior traders must be taxed as salaried employees rather than profit-sharing members of the partnership. The judgment, citation [2026] UKSC 18 in case UKSC/2025/0028, was delivered by five justices — Briggs, Hamblen, Burrows, Richards, and Simler.

The dispute turned on the Salaried Members Rules, part of the Income Tax (Trading and Other Income) Act 2005 as amended in 2014, and their three-part test of conditions A, B, and C. HMRC's position, upheld by the court, was that the members in question received fixed compensation without genuine reference to firm profits and lacked sufficient influence over the LLP to fall outside the rules. Founder Michael Platt — 12th on the 2026 Sunday Times Rich List at around £13 billion — had argued they were true profit-sharing members. The fight has run through the First-Tier Tribunal, the Upper Tribunal, and a January 2025 Court of Appeal ruling that already narrowed the "significant influence" test to legally enforceable rights.

The ruling exposes BlueCrest to a combined liability approaching £200 million for the 2014/15 to 2018/19 tax years and hands HMRC a precedent to press similar cases against other LLPs. Interveners included the Alternative Investment Management Association and the Managed Funds Association. "Firms need a stable and predictable environment to make long-term investment decisions," said Rob Hailey of the Managed Funds Association.

### UK Supreme Court rules on issue estoppel in Danish tax authority's cum-ex case against ED&F Man

The UK Supreme Court handed down judgment on 1 July 2026 in Skatteforvaltningen — the Danish Customs and Tax Administration, or SKAT — v MCML Ltd, formerly ED&F Man Capital Markets Ltd, citation [2026] UKSC 19 in case UKSC/2025/0090, with Lord Sales giving the lead judgment alongside Lord Lloyd-Jones, Lady Rose, Lady Simler, and Lord Doherty.

The case turned on issue estoppel: whether SKAT's 2022 fraudulent-misrepresentation claim was barred by an earlier 2018 negligence claim against the same defendant. SKAT alleged ED&F Man made misrepresentations that induced improper dividend tax refunds under "cum-ex" dividend-arbitrage schemes that defrauded the Danish treasury — part of a wider effort by SKAT to recover over £1.5 billion from more than 100 defendants over refunds it says it wrongly paid between 2012 and 2015. The 2018 claim had been struck out under the "Revenue Rule," which bars English courts from enforcing a foreign state's tax claims; MCML argued SKAT was estopped from re-advancing matters decided or decidable in 2018, and the Court of Appeal had agreed.

The Supreme Court addressed the scope and limits of issue estoppel where the earlier claim failed on a jurisdictional or procedural ground rather than on the merits — a distinction that carries weight for the run of transnational cum-ex litigation still moving through the English courts, including SKAT's separate £1.4 billion claim against other defendants that the High Court dismissed after a 138-day trial in October 2025. The appeal was heard on 11–12 February 2026.
