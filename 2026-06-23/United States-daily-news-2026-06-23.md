### U.S. opens 60-day window for Iranian oil trade, unlocking billions for Tehran

The U.S. Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued General License X on or about June 22, 2026, authorizing a 60-day window for Iranian crude oil trade and the shipping, insurance, and financial transactions that move it. The carve-out runs through 12:01 a.m. EDT on August 21, 2026, and ranks as the most sweeping easing of U.S. energy sanctions on Iran in years. CNBC, reporting June 23, described the waivers as unlocking "billions in revenue for Tehran," a country whose oil exports have been choked by sanctions since the U.S. abandoned the 2015 nuclear deal in 2018.

The license flows from the Islamabad Memorandum, a framework signed June 17, 2026 that paused the recent Iran conflict and reopened the Strait of Hormuz to commercial traffic. The strait carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil; its closure during the fighting had spiked crude prices and rattled shippers. Under the framework, Washington pledged immediate oil waivers in exchange for Iranian steps toward a final agreement, with Qatar and Pakistan mediating talks at the Swiss resort of Buergenstock.

The relief is conditional. The 60-day clock is tied to Iran's compliance with the memorandum's terms, and the window can be allowed to lapse if Tehran balks. For global markets, the immediate consequence is the prospect of sanctioned Iranian barrels returning to legal trade for the first time in years, easing a supply picture that the strait crisis had tightened.

### U.S. jobless rate holds at 4.3% as states report May data, Fed weighs a hike

National nonfarm payrolls added about 172,000 jobs in May and the unemployment rate held at 4.3%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics confirmed when it released its State Employment and Unemployment summary at 10:00 a.m. ET on June 23, 2026. The monthly report breaks the labor market down across all 50 states and the District of Columbia. May's gains clustered in leisure and hospitality, local government, and health care; financial activities shed jobs. The national rate has sat in a narrow 4.3% to 4.5% band since July 2025, a picture of a labor market that is slowing without breaking.

The data lands inside a tightening, not easing, monetary backdrop. At its June 17 meeting — the first chaired by Kevin Warsh — the Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously to hold the federal funds rate at 3.5–3.75%, with annualized CPI inflation running at 4.2%, lifted partly by the energy spike from the Iran conflict. The Fed's own projections showed nine of 18 members penciling in a rate hike before year-end. Warsh stripped the post-meeting statement of language signaling a bias toward cuts and said he would not offer markets forward guidance, leaving a later-2026 hike on the table as the steady payroll numbers give the Fed room to lean against inflation rather than growth.

### Alan Greenspan, Fed chairman who shaped the Great Moderation, dies at 100

Alan Greenspan, the 13th Chairman of the Federal Reserve, died June 22, 2026 at his home in Washington, D.C., aged 100, from complications of Parkinson's disease. His wife of 29 years, NBC News correspondent Andrea Mitchell, confirmed the death. Greenspan ran the central bank from August 1987 to January 2006 under Reagan, George H.W. Bush, Clinton, and George W. Bush — 19 years across four presidencies and one of the longest U.S. expansions on record.

Bloomberg Opinion columnist John Authers, writing June 23, framed the legacy as "as convoluted as his words." Greenspan presided over the Great Moderation, a long stretch of low inflation and steady growth that made him "the Maestro." But his opposition to derivatives regulation and the low-rate policy he ran after the 2001 downturn are widely read as preconditions for the 2008 financial crisis. Greenspan himself, testifying after the crash, conceded he had found a "flaw" in his free-market ideology. His December 1996 warning of "irrational exuberance" in asset prices, and his 2007 memoir "The Age of Turbulence," bracket a career remembered for both its prescience and its blind spots.

Authers draws the contrast with the present. Greenspan's defining problem was talking markets down from froth; current Fed Chair Kevin Warsh faces the opposite bind — rates pinned at 3.5–3.75% with CPI at 4.2%, fighting to pull inflation down rather than coax growth up. The Federal Reserve marked the passing "with deep sadness," saying his contributions "left a lasting mark on this institution."

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu pressed for full access by the IAEA, the UN nuclear watchdog, to Iranian sites — the verification mechanism that Trump claims to have secured and that Iran now says was never promised. The same framework underpins the 60-day U.S. oil-sanctions waiver issued June 22, making Tehran's compliance, or its denial, the hinge on which both the inspections question and billions in restored oil revenue turn.

## 4. Society & Livelihood

### What ending birthright citizenship would mean for 4.4 million U.S.-born children

A Supreme Court ruling in Trump v. Barbara could redraw who counts as American at birth, and an NPR explainer published June 23, 2026 lays out the stakes as the decision nears. The case turns on the 14th Amendment's citizenship clause — "all persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof" — and on Executive Order 14160, which directs federal agencies not to recognize citizenship for children born to parents who are in the country unlawfully or only temporarily. Roughly 4.4 million U.S.-born children of undocumented parents currently live in the United States; if the order stands, future births in that category could be stripped of automatic citizenship, raising the prospect of statelessness.

The legal weight runs against the order. In United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898), the Court held that a child born on U.S. soil to non-citizen parents was a citizen — the precedent that has anchored birthright citizenship for more than a century. The justices heard oral arguments April 1, 2026, and reporting from the courtroom suggested a majority skeptical of the government's position. A ruling is expected by the end of June 2026; June 23 was an opinion-announcement day, but the decision had not been released at publication.

### Ending birthright citizenship would open an immediate newborn coverage gap, bioethicist warns

About 9% of all U.S. births are to parents who are undocumented or hold temporary visas — the exact group whose children would lose citizenship if the Supreme Court upholds Executive Order 14160 in Trump v. Barbara. Writing in STAT News on June 23, 2026, bioethicist Rachel E. Fabi of SUNY Upstate Medical University traces what that would mean for the health system, not just the legal one.

The pressure point is Medicaid. The program covers roughly 40% of all U.S. births and automatically extends coverage to newborns for their first year — but that automatic coverage is keyed to the infant's citizenship. Strip the citizenship and the coverage vanishes at birth, creating an immediate gap for exactly the families least able to absorb it. Fabi argues the chilling effect is already measurable: 43% of likely undocumented immigrants report a child in their household skipped necessary healthcare in the past year out of fear of immigration enforcement. A ruling against birthright citizenship, she writes, would compound that avoidance, reaching school-based health services and routine pediatric care for U.S.-born children swept into legal limbo.

## 5. Corporate IPO & M&A

### AbbVie to buy Apogee Therapeutics for $10.9 billion to challenge Dupixent

AbbVie (NYSE: ABBV) agreed June 22, 2026 to acquire Apogee Therapeutics for $135.11 per share in cash, an equity value of roughly $10.9 billion, in a bet on the next generation of immunology drugs. Both boards approved the deal unanimously; it is expected to close in Q3 2026, subject to an Apogee shareholder vote and Hart-Scott-Rodino antitrust clearance. AbbVie says the acquisition becomes accretive to adjusted diluted earnings per share from 2032.

The prize is Apogee's lead candidate, zumilokibart (APG777), an anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibody in Phase 2/3 trials for atopic dermatitis and asthma. Its pitch is convenience: longer-term data support maintenance dosing as infrequent as twice a year, a potential edge over rivals that require more frequent injections. Apogee's broader pipeline includes APG990, an anti-IL-4Rα antibody, and APG808, an anti-TSLP candidate.

The target is Dupixent, the Sanofi/Regeneron blockbuster that pulls in roughly $15 billion a year treating the same inflammatory diseases. AbbVie needs a new immunology engine: its old anchor, Humira, has lost patent exclusivity to biosimilars, and the company now leans on Skyrizi and Rinvoq for growth. Buying Apogee deepens that bench and gives AbbVie a direct shot at the atopic-dermatitis and asthma markets Dupixent dominates.
