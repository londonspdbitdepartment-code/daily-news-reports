### China launches record €5 billion sovereign euro bond sale across three tranches

China's Ministry of Finance opened books on June 26, 2026 for a €5 billion (US$5.7 billion) sovereign euro bond, the largest euro-denominated issuance in the country's history. A term sheet seen by Reuters split the deal into three tranches: five-year notes at mid-swaps plus 3 basis points, eight-year notes at plus 12, and twelve-year notes at plus 22. Settlement is set for July 3, 2026.

Beijing has been pushing back into euro markets at an accelerating pace. After a four-year absence, it sold €2 billion in September 2024, then returned in November 2025 with a €4 billion deal in Luxembourg that drew more than €100 billion of orders — roughly 25 times the amount on offer, with European and Asian investors taking the bulk. The €5 billion launched today raises that ambition by another quarter and again routes through Luxembourg, where the finance ministry had said it planned to sell up to €5 billion in the week of June 22.

Sixteen banks were mandated, a syndicate that spans the geopolitical divide: Bank of China, Bank of Communications, Agricultural Bank of China, China Construction Bank (Asia), CICC and ICBC alongside US houses BofA Securities, Citigroup, Goldman Sachs (Asia) and JPMorgan, plus European lenders Credit Agricole CIB, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, Société Générale, Standard Chartered Bank and UBS. The presence of the four largest US banks signals that appetite for Chinese sovereign paper in Europe has not been dented by Washington-Beijing frictions.

The pricing comes as the yuan softens. The offshore yuan weakened to around 6.80 per dollar on Friday, on track for a second consecutive weekly loss, as the dollar firmed after the Federal Reserve turned more hawkish — markets are now pricing a 75% probability of a September rate hike.

### NPC Standing Committee adopts revised Trademark Law and tougher CPA Law

China's 14th National People's Congress Standing Committee closed its 23rd session on Friday June 26, 2026 after convening June 23-26, approving three bills. The headline measure is the fifth revision of the Trademark Law — the most significant overhaul of the regime in more than a decade. It extends protection to non-traditional marks, builds in anti-bad-faith mechanisms, grants the China National Intellectual Property Administration (CNIPA) proactive cancellation powers, and raises penalties on agents who facilitate abusive filings.

The revision matters for foreign brands, which have long battled trademark squatting in China. The new text gives CNIPA statutory grounds to refuse or cancel applications filed without genuine intent to use, that clearly exceed normal business needs, or that come from applicants with a documented pattern of bad-faith registrations — converting what was a discretionary case-by-case judgment into a structured enforcement framework. Brand owners do face one tightening: the window to oppose a published mark is being compressed. The bill follows a public consultation that ran from December 27, 2025 to February 9, 2026 and approval in principle by the State Council on March 13, 2026.

The session also passed an amendment to the Certified Public Accountants Law that doubles the maximum fine for false audit reports to 10 times the gains accrued through fraud, up from 5x, adds business suspensions, licence revocations and practice bans, and extends liability to parties who collude with accountants. The change deepens Beijing's capital-markets anti-fraud campaign that intensified after audit failures tied to Evergrande. A third bill, an amendment to the Lawyers Law, was sent out for public comment, while draft Finance Law, People's Bank of China, Government Procurement and Bid Invitation laws were reviewed but not passed.

### China issues first code of conduct for AI smart glasses after covert-filming outrage

The China Academy of Information and Communications Technology (CAICT) released the industry's first code of conduct for AI smart glasses on Thursday June 25, responding to public anger over users covertly filming strangers with the increasingly popular devices. The voluntary code, issued by the research institute under the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, calls on manufacturers to adopt a "minimum data collection" approach, provide clear indicators when cameras or microphones are active, and obtain explicit user consent before recording.

The trigger was a stream of clips tied to Hangzhou-based Rokid. Videos posted on the company's own user forum showed members of the public being secretly recorded on subway trains, in parks, on beaches and in shopping malls; one widely shared clip caught a flight attendant serving meals, apparently unaware she was being filmed. A separate post in late May showed a photo of two Spring Airlines flight attendants captioned approvingly, amplifying the backlash. Earlier this month Rokid removed some of the videos and blocked the accounts that uploaded them; the company did not immediately respond to a request for comment on Friday.

The episode exposed a hardware loophole the new code only partly addresses. Low-cost stickers sold online — more than 5,000 units by some platform counts — can cover the recording-indicator LED without tripping the sensors meant to disable filming when the light is obscured, making covert capture hard for bystanders to detect. The privacy debate mirrors the "glasshole" controversy dogging Meta's Ray-Ban smart glasses in the US, but China is first to publish a sector-wide governance standard.

### Goldman Sachs lifts China EV export forecast as carmakers consolidate overseas footing

Goldman Sachs raised its China passenger-vehicle export forecasts by 6 to 11 per cent across 2026 to 2030 in a research note released Thursday June 25, now projecting 7.8 million cars shipped in 2030. The bank cited accelerating new-energy vehicle exports, writing that "China's new-energy vehicle exports have been accelerating, with 63 per cent year-on-year growth in the first quarter of 2026, driven by increasing supply of competitive models." Excluding mainland China and the US, it expects Chinese-branded cars to take 17 per cent of the global market by 2030.

The upgrade reflects how overseas shipments are cushioning Chinese automakers including BYD and Xpeng against softening domestic deliveries. That cushion is already sizeable: China's overall passenger-car exports hit 922,000 units in 2025, up 29 per cent, and BYD alone shipped more than one million vehicles abroad last year, more than double its 2024 total, with overseas sales briefly overtaking domestic volumes for the first time.

Goldman's call echoes JPMorgan, which said last month that Chinese cars would post a 150 per cent sales surge in western Europe over the three years to 2028, grabbing a 20 per cent share in markets like Germany, Italy, France and the UK with annual deliveries of 2.5 million units — up from the 1 million units Chinese carmakers sold across western Europe last year. The forecasts come despite EU tariffs of up to 35.3 per cent on Chinese EVs imposed in October 2024, which manufacturers have largely sidestepped by shifting to plug-in hybrids that escape the duties.

## 5. China-Nexus Finance & Investment

### China wields rare-earth dominance against Japan as G7 plots price-floor stockpile

China has halted shipments of heavy rare earths to Japan since January 2026, with only minimal flows through May, weaponising its mineral dominance in what Bloomberg describes as the two countries' worst geopolitical dispute in years. Beijing formally announced export restrictions on January 6, 2026, targeting dual-use technologies including rare earth elements, permanent magnets and other critical minerals — a response to Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's November 2025 remarks linking a potential attack on Taiwan to Japan's existential security.

The squeeze is biting. Shipments of dysprosium, terbium and gallium have effectively stopped, and the price of neodymium-praseodymium oxide rose sixfold between January and June 2026. The leverage is structural: China controls roughly 90% of global rare-earth magnet production and refining, and Japan imported over 5.2 million kilograms of rare-earth metals from China in 2024, 63% of its total rare-earth imports. Chinese customs data confirmed negligible dysprosium, terbium and yttrium oxide shipments to Japan from late 2025 onward — the inputs needed for the high-performance NdFeB magnets used in EVs, motors, wind turbines and defence systems.

Tokyo is now trying to build an alternative architecture. In June 2026 Japan proposed a G7 coordinated stockpiling initiative anchored by price floors, with the Lynas-JARE agreement setting $110/kg on 5,000 metric tons as a reference floor for neodymium and praseodymium through 2038. G7 leaders agreed to coordinate stockpiling to push dependence on any single non-G7 supplier below 60% by 2030. The price-floor mechanism is designed to keep non-Chinese mines economic even if Beijing floods the market to undercut them — the tactic that historically killed Western rare-earth projects.

### Pax Silica Summit adds nine signatories including the EU to China-exclusionary AI supply-chain bloc

Nine new members joined the Pax Silica Declaration as the second summit of the US-led technology alliance wrapped up June 26, 2026 at the State Department in Washington, expanding the China-exclusionary bloc to more than 20 countries. Argentina, Germany, the Netherlands, Chile, Costa Rica, Greece, Kazakhstan, Panama and the European Union signed during the two-day meeting (June 25-26), presided over by Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau and Under Secretary of Economic Affairs Jacob Helberg. Taiwan took part as a non-signatory through Deputy Minister of Digital Affairs Isabel Hou.

Pax Silica, launched in December 2025 with seven founding signatories including Japan, South Korea, the Netherlands, the UK and Australia, aims to secure supply chains across semiconductors, AI and rare earths while reducing reliance on China. This summit moved it from declaration to instruments. It launched the Declaration on AI Opportunity, committing members to pro-growth AI policies, trusted technology ecosystems, resilient supply chains and infrastructure investment; PaxPass, a cargo verification system with AI-powered risk assessment and expedited customs clearance backed by $50 million in US foreign assistance; and Foundry School, a workforce program run with Stanford University.

Landau framed the logic as collective dependence: "No single nation holds every link in these technologies' supply chains, and that is our strength, not our weakness." The members explicitly committed to "reducing excessive dependencies, including on China." Not all participants were aligned — India raised concerns on the sidelines about an AI "kill switch" provision.

### China markets policing and arms-control model to Africa's Great Lakes region

China is pitching its strict policing and arms-control expertise as a template to stabilise Africa's conflict-torn Great Lakes region, trading on its reputation as one of the world's safest countries. Earlier this month, China and the United Nations hosted security officials from Tanzania, Uganda, Burundi, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Rwanda at the People's Public Security University of China in Beijing for a training program on the control of small arms and light weapons.

The training-of-trainers initiative aims to strengthen these states' capacity to manage weapons, curb illicit circulation and support the African Union's "Silencing the Guns in Africa" project. Like the Sahel, the Great Lakes has long been destabilised by the proliferation of small arms that fuels conflict and erodes local security.

That instability is not an abstract concern for Beijing. The region hosts vast Chinese economic interests, above all its copper and cobalt mining operations in the DR Congo — minerals central to the EV and electronics supply chains. By exporting a security model alongside its mining footprint, China is extending the resource-security playbook that pairs commercial presence with governance influence across the continent.

### China tests BeiDou-linked tracking device to secure China-Europe railway cargo

A China Railway team has tested a satellite-linked device mounted on shipping containers that uses the BeiDou Navigation Satellite system to track cargo in real time along the China-Europe railway, aiming to close long-standing security and monitoring gaps on the transcontinental route. The device enables real-time positioning and data collection, and researchers say it could better protect containers against theft and loss on the vital land corridor.

"Leveraging the autonomous and controllable characteristics of BeiDou-3, it achieves all-weather, accurate perception of container location and status," the team wrote in a paper published in the journal Railway Logistics in April. The researchers — drawn from China Railway Chengdu Group, the China Academy of Railway Sciences and the China Railway Container Transport Corporation (CRCT) — tested the device on both domestic and international sections of the China Railway Express, checking transmission integrity, battery life and satellite lock-on time.

The push to tighten monitoring comes as the corridor faces scrutiny. The flagship service has surpassed 130,000 freight-train trips and moved more than $520 billion of goods since 2011, and the route has previously drawn criticism over empty containers and cargo integrity. A BeiDou-based tracking layer also advances Beijing's wider goal of substituting its homegrown satellite system for GPS in critical logistics infrastructure.
