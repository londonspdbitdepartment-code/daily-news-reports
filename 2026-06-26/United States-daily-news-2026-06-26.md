### University of Michigan consumer sentiment revised up to 49.5, still 19% below a year ago

The University of Michigan lifted its final June reading of consumer sentiment to 49.5 on June 26, up from the 48.9 preliminary figure released June 12 and a 9.2% gain over May's record low of 44.8. The improvement still left the headline index 19.4% below June 2025's 60.7, and it undershot the 50.0 consensus forecast.

May's 44.8 was the worst reading in the survey's history and the third straight monthly decline, driven by cost-of-living fears as Strait of Hormuz disruptions pushed gasoline prices higher. June's partial recovery came from the reverse: the director's statement attributed the bounce to "early-month easing in gasoline prices," and called the gain "widespread, seen across age, education, and political party." Lower-income households, for whom fuel is a bigger share of spending, posted the strongest improvement.

Inflation expectations softened. Consumers see prices rising 4.6% over the year ahead, down from 4.8% in May, and 3.4% over the long run, down from 3.9%. The survey nonetheless described overall views of the economy as "still relatively dour," with respondents fixed on kitchen-table costs.

### Treasury yields little changed as oil slides and traders price a 68% chance of a September hike

The 10-year Treasury yield eased to about 4.45% on Friday, a second straight session of declines, as falling oil prices outweighed fresh tension in the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran struck a Singapore-flagged cargo ship the day before. Cooling crude trimmed inflation worries even as traders braced for the Fed's preferred gauge.

Those worries are real. Data released June 25 showed the core PCE price index up 3.4% year-over-year and headline PCE up 4.1%, gaining 0.4% on the month. The Federal Open Market Committee, under Chair Kevin Warsh, voted unanimously on June 17 to hold the benchmark rate at 3.5%–3.75%, and lifted its 2026 outlook to 3.6% headline and 3.3% core PCE, well above the 2.7% pace officials had penciled in back in March.

The hawkish shift has flipped the rate debate from cuts to hikes. Markets now assign a roughly 68% probability that the Fed raises rates in September, up from 29% a week earlier. Stocks fell on the day as a tech sell-off compounded rate pressure: the S&P 500 dropped 0.75%, the Nasdaq Composite 1.25%, and the Dow 0.44%.

### Apple and Microsoft raise hardware prices as AI demand quadruples memory chip costs

Apple raised prices by as much as 25% across its Mac and iPad lines on June 26, blaming a surge in memory and storage costs tied to AI data-center demand. The base MacBook Air jumped from $1,099 to $1,299, the MacBook Pro from $1,699 to $1,999, the iPad Air from $599 to $749, and the Mac Studio M3 Ultra took the steepest hit, from $3,999 to $5,299. An Apple spokesperson said "The rapid expansion of AI data centres has created an extraordinary surge in demand for memory and storage."

Microsoft followed with Xbox increases effective August 1, lifting the Series S to $500 and the Series X to as much as $800, and warned that console memory and storage prices have already risen more than 2.5x. DRAM and NAND flash costs have quadrupled since 2025 as manufacturers divert capacity to high-bandwidth memory for AI servers.

The price moves triggered a regional sell-off. Apple shares fell more than 6%. South Korea's SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics each dropped more than 10% at one point, Japan's Kioxia sank as much as 14%, and the MSCI Asia Pacific Infotech gauge lost 6.4%. The S&P 500 fell 0.75% and the Nasdaq 1.25% on the day.

### IAEA chief demands full access to Iran's nuclear sites as Tehran resists inspections

IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi called on June 26 for "a very strong system of verification" of Iran's nuclear programme, demanding full access to its sites. Speaking at a news conference in Japan, Grossi said "There is a bit of a war of statements here," adding "We must verify everywhere" and "There is an agreement and to comply with that agreement, the IAEA will have to have access and inspect."

The dispute centers on a 14-point memorandum of understanding signed remotely on June 17 by President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. Washington calls inspections "fully agreed on"; Tehran disagrees. Iranian Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said on June 25 there were "no plans to grant access to inspectors" for now, tying any access to the lifting of U.S. sanctions. Trump dismissed Iran's "protestations and false statements."

The stakes are concrete. Iran suspended IAEA cooperation after U.S. and Israeli strikes on its nuclear infrastructure in June 2025; limited access resumed in September. The agency estimates Iran held 440.9 kg of uranium enriched to up to 60% before the conflict — material that remains unaccounted for, with inspectors still barred from the bombed sites. Grossi said technical work on inspection arrangements has begun.

### US mobilizes $150 million for Venezuela earthquake, waives sanctions for relief through October

Venezuela's confirmed earthquake death toll climbed to at least 589 by June 26, up from 235 a day earlier, with more than 4,300 injured, acting President Delcy Rodríguez said. Twin quakes of magnitude 7.2 and 7.5 struck near San Felipe in western Venezuela at 18:04 local time on June 25, potentially affecting some 6.76 million people. Rodríguez declared a nationwide state of emergency and pledged "We are going to rescue the people who are trapped. We are working tirelessly."

The United States moved to help despite years of sanctions on Caracas. The State Department said it was mobilizing $150 million in aid, including a $100 million contribution to a UN humanitarian fund, while Secretary of State Marco Rubio deployed a Disaster Assistance Response Team and urban search-and-rescue crews from Fairfax County, Virginia, and Los Angeles. U.S. Southern Command directed naval assets including the amphibious transport USS Fort Lauderdale (LPD 28) and the littoral combat ship USS Billings (LCS 15).

To clear the legal path, the Treasury's OFAC issued Venezuela General License 60 on June 25, authorizing all earthquake-relief transactions from June 26 through October 23, carving a humanitarian corridor through the existing sanctions regime. Rescue teams arrived from Spain, Germany, Chile, and Switzerland, with further pledges from Turkey, China, Qatar, Brazil, Portugal, and Canada.

### The "tokenmaxxing" era ends as enterprises switch to cost-efficient AI, pressuring OpenAI and Anthropic

The era of "tokenmaxxing" — paying developers to maximize AI token consumption regardless of return — is fading across corporate America, threatening revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic, the two frontier labs that profited most from the spend-at-all-costs mindset, CNBC reported June 26. Both companies, whose valuations approach $1 trillion, filed confidentially for IPOs in early June.

The shift is concrete. Lindy CEO Flo Crivello told CNBC his startup moved 100% of its API traffic from Anthropic's Claude models to DeepSeek, a Chinese open-weight provider, after the switch caused costs to "crash to the ground" — a move he said would save millions within months. Uber introduced AI spending tiers in June starting at $1,500 per employee per month, after disclosing in April that it had burned through its entire annual AI budget in four months. Accenture is restricting employees from depleting token reserves on basic tasks like turning PDFs into slides; its agentic AI strategy lead Justice Kwak said "We're hitting this inflection point where AI is becoming material to the cost structure. Spend is becoming very unpredictable" — a reversal from the firm's earlier warning that staff who avoided AI risked losing promotions.

Microsoft, Amazon, and Google are pushing cheaper, efficiency-focused models into the gap. D.A. Davidson analyst Gil Luria warned that "some of their largest enterprise customers may start limiting their out-of-control token spend," directly threatening the two labs' annualized growth. The watchword is shifting from tokenmaxxing to "valuemaxxing."

### AI memory chip shortage drives up electronics prices, squeezing retailers before the holidays

Data centers consumed an estimated 70% of global memory production in 2026, leaving smartphone, PC, and consumer-electronics makers fighting over the remaining three chips in every ten and forcing retailers to raise prices, cut discounts, push lower-capacity models, or absorb thinner margins heading into the holiday season, CNBC reported June 26. Memory prices have more than doubled since October 2025, with DRAM contract prices up about 64% quarter-on-quarter in Q2 2026 and NAND up about 60%.

The pass-through to shoppers is already visible. Memory now accounts for roughly 35% of the materials cost of an HP laptop, up from 15–18% a quarter earlier. IDC expects consumer PC prices to rise about 17% and smartphone prices about 13% versus 2025 levels, with no return to normal expected until the end of 2027. Apple has already raised MacBook and iPad prices in multiple markets.

The three dominant memory makers — Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix — have pivoted output toward AI chipmakers such as Nvidia, booking record profits while starving the consumer supply chain. The squeeze is forecast to cut global PC shipments by 10.4% and smartphone shipments by 8.4% this year.

### Former park rangers teach a cancelled Black history exhibit as "Resistance Rangers"

Former National Park ranger Elizabeth Kerwin spent years building a "wall of remembrance" honoring hundreds of enslaved people tied to Harpers Ferry National Historic Park in West Virginia, only to see the Trump administration cancel the exhibit; the building meant to house it now sits empty, windows boarded up. It is one of dozens of displays scrubbed from federal lands as the country approaches its 250th anniversary.

In response, former rangers formed a group calling themselves "Resistance Rangers" and helped found an education coalition named "America 433+," a nod to the 433 sites in the National Park System. They chose Juneteenth, June 19, to launch a public-education campaign, printing copies of banned pamphlets and bringing the material directly to park visitors. Melissa Dalley, a Resistance Ranger and former guide at the Martin Van Buren National Historic Site in New York, described the timing as a nod to Black history and the speed of its removal from public sites.

On June 26 the group announced plans to protest at a national demonstration of Trump's 250th celebration, where organizers intend to gather signatures on a "declaration of interdependence" advocating "safety, dignity, living wages and access to a clean environment for all." At a Juneteenth event they handed out wooden "junior Resistance Ranger" badges and workbooks discontinued by the administration.

### Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool liner cut with a "sharp knife or razor," seven arrested

The National Park Service disclosed in a June 26 court filing that the liner along the bottom of the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool was sliced with a "sharp knife or razor," damaging the foam sealant installed during a $16 million rehabilitation. U.S. Park Police first responded to a complaint on June 9. After "recurring cases and videos of people ripping at the coating began to circulate," the service concluded "this was not an isolated incident, but a new trend to attempt to damage the Reflecting Pool."

Staff also found that fencing around the pool "had been forcibly removed and thrown into the pool." As of June 26 the Interior Department reported seven arrests, seven federal citations, and 18 police reports tied to the alleged damage. President Trump called the vandals "deranged."

The pool has had a rough month. Beyond the cuts, an algae bloom turned the water fluorescent green, and the day before the filing a power outage hit the food hall of Trump's "Great American State Fair" on the National Mall nearby. The $16 million renovation, which added the foam liner and an "American Flag Blue" surface coating, was completed only in early June.

### Former NOAA staff launch Climate.us to restore a data site shut down by the Trump administration

Former NOAA employees launched a nonprofit site, Climate.us, to fill the void left when the Trump administration shut Climate.gov in 2025 as part of DOGE cutbacks, NPR reported June 26. Visitors to the old government page now see a notice that products "previously housed under Climate.gov" have been moved elsewhere; the underlying data technically survives on federal servers, but project head Rebecca Lindsey, a former Climate.gov program director, said it is now "difficult to find."

Lindsey began rebuilding in August 2025 with two other former NOAA colleagues. The team crowdsourced roughly $280,000 for development and secured one anonymous grant to fund operations through February 2027. The new site recreates the old climate dashboard with more than 15 key graphs, plus a 15-year news archive, expert blogs, maps, and classroom materials — restoring content that drew nearly one million monthly visitors to Climate.gov as recently as 2021. About 80 scientists have volunteered for its science panel.

Lindsey said the editorial stance will stay "nonpartisan but will be focusing on the science." Texas Tech climate scientist Katharine Hayhoe praised the effort for helping "people connect what's happening at the global scale to how it matters," and Gretchen Gehrke of the Environmental Data & Governance Initiative called it "a success story" of expertise moving outside government.

### Onsemi to buy Synaptics in $7 billion all-stock deal, its largest ever

ON Semiconductor agreed on June 25 to acquire Synaptics in an all-stock deal with an enterprise value of about $7 billion — the largest acquisition in onsemi's history. Each Synaptics share converts into 1.350 onsemi shares, a fixed ratio implying roughly a 19% premium to both stocks' 10-day volume-weighted average prices, and leaving Synaptics holders with about 12% of the combined company. Both boards approved unanimously.

The logic is a bet on "physical AI" — inference running on devices at the edge rather than in the cloud. Onsemi supplies power and sensing chips for automotive and industrial systems; Synaptics brings edge-compute processors, wireless connectivity, and human-machine interface technology. Onsemi said the deal expands its addressable market by $30 billion to $243 billion by 2030, targets $200 million in annual synergies, and should add to non-GAAP earnings per share within 18 months of closing.

The transaction is expected to close in mid-2027, pending Synaptics shareholder and regulatory approval, with the two companies operating independently until then.

### Trump's Great American State Fair opens on the National Mall for the US 250th anniversary

President Trump opened the Great American State Fair on the National Mall on June 25, kicking off the country's 250th-anniversary celebrations with a free 16-day event running through July 10. Organized by Freedom 250, it features a giant Ferris wheel, rodeo demonstrations, fireworks, and exhibits from all 56 states and territories along the Mall between 4th and 14th Streets. "Tonight, as we stand on the edge of our 250th year of independence, I am thrilled to declare that America is back," Trump told supporters.

The launch was bumpy. More than half the musical acts originally booked pulled out beforehand, some calling the event overtly partisan, and The Hill noted Trump "stuck to the script" in unusually restrained remarks. On June 26 a power outage in the food hall melted dessert vendors' ice cream, and the algae bloom that turned the nearby Reflecting Pool fluorescent green added to the visual trouble.

The fair also carries a MAHA food component run through the Agriculture Department, with Secretary Brooke Rollins and Ambassador Monica Crowley joining multigenerational farm families to showcase American agriculture.

### National Geographic Museum of Exploration opens in Washington in $300 million campus revamp

The National Geographic Museum of Exploration opened to the public on June 26 at the Society's reimagined campus at 1600 M Street NW in downtown Washington, capping a four-year, $300-million-plus revitalization of the institution founded in 1888. The museum offers more than 100,000 square feet of public space — a sixfold expansion — and houses over 650 "Explorer objects" alongside interactive exhibits drawn from the work of more than 15,000 National Geographic Explorers.

The 9 a.m. ribbon-cutting drew filmmaker and deep-sea explorer James Cameron, who said "The beautiful thing about exploration is, you've gotta tell the tale when you come back." Society CEO Jill Tiefenthaler called the museum "where our legacy of storytelling meets the experiential technology of today, inviting everyone to step into the worlds of National Geographic Explorers." Permanent exhibits include Photo Ark: Animals of Earth, the Rolex Explorers Landing, The Archives, and a near-400-seat theater.

Admission starts at $29.99 for adults, $25.99 for seniors, and $22.99 for youth aged 5–17, with children four and under free; opening-weekend tickets were nearly sold out. The Society also launched a citywide "Yellow Borders" outdoor selfie-frame campaign at eight DMV-area locations through July 5, and is seeking LEED Platinum certification.
