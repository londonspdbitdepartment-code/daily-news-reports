### EU budget chief Serafin attacks frugal states as MFF fight opens in Brussels

Budget and Administration Commissioner Piotr Serafin used the European Commission's annual budget conference on July 2 to round on the bloc's self-styled "frugal" governments — the Netherlands, Austria, Denmark and Sweden — over their push to slim down the next Multiannual Financial Framework. The gathering, staged under the theme "Boost Europe: Leveraging the EU Budget for strategic priorities", turned into an early skirmish in what will be the most contentious EU budget negotiation in years.

The stakes are large. The Commission's July 2025 proposal for the 2028–2034 MFF runs to nearly €2 trillion, well above the current seven-year budget, and folds cohesion funds, farm subsidies and migration spending into single national plans for each member state. Poorer eastern and southern regions fear the restructuring will squeeze the cohesion money they depend on; a Bulgarian minister told the conference there must be "no further cuts on cohesion".

The speaker list underscored how much of the EU's firepower now hangs on the outcome: Eurogroup President Kyriakos Pierrakakis, EIB President Nadia Calviño, Defence Commissioner Andrius Kubilius, and UNDP head and former Belgian premier Alexander De Croo all took the floor. Unanimity among all 27 states is required to pass the framework, and the frugal bloc's insistence on cuts sets up a two-year standoff before the money starts flowing in 2028.

### Euro-area under-35s hold 82% less net wealth than the adult average, ECB survey shows

A typical euro-area household headed by someone aged 16 to 34 holds median net wealth of just €24,600 — 82% below the €140,100 median for adults overall, according to the latest ECB Household Finance and Consumption Survey. The gap quantifies a divide that has hardened across a generation locked out of the asset that drives most European wealth: property.

Where a young household lives determines almost everything. Dutch under-35s sit at the top with €73,000 in median net wealth, more than ten times the €6,900 held by their Bulgarian peers at the bottom. The spread tracks differences in home-ownership rates, inheritance flows and the depth of local housing and pension markets.

Fabian Pfeffer, a professor at LMU Munich who studies wealth inequality, frames the numbers as a structural rather than temporary problem: young people increasingly rely on family transfers to build assets, which entrenches inequality across generations rather than closing it. With house prices across much of the euro area still near record highs relative to incomes, the survey points to a cohort that will start its wealth-building years far behind where its parents stood at the same age.

### Spain's registered unemployment falls below 2.3 million for the first time since 2008

Spain's registered unemployment dropped to 2,291,982 in June 2026, falling below 2.3 million for the first time since January 2008 — before the financial crisis wiped out a decade of Spanish job creation. The figure lands alongside a Social Security contributor count of 22.47 million, a record, confirming a labour market running hotter than at any point in the post-crisis era.

The recovery reaches groups that were slowest to benefit in earlier upswings. Female unemployment fell to 1.39 million, the lowest since August 2008, while youth unemployment dropped to 159,800. Foreign-worker affiliations climbed to 3.45 million, reflecting how heavily migrant labour now underpins Spanish tourism, agriculture and construction.

Deputy Prime Minister Yolanda Díaz cast the data as vindication of the government's labour reforms, which curbed temporary contracts and strengthened collective bargaining. The milestone caps a run in which Spain has repeatedly outpaced the larger euro-area economies on job growth, even as Germany and France stagnate.

### Renewables supplied 45.5% of EU electricity in Q1, with Denmark and Portugal out front

Renewable sources generated 45.5% of the EU's electricity in the first quarter of 2026, up from 42.7% a year earlier, according to Eurostat. Wind did the heavy lifting at 44.9% of the renewable mix, followed by hydro at 28% and solar at 17.3%, as the bloc pushes to sever its dependence on imported fossil fuels.

The national spread is stark. Denmark led at 90%, followed by Portugal at 82.9% and Lithuania at 75.7% — countries that have leaned hardest into wind and hydro. At the other end sit Czechia at 12.7%, Malta at 13% and Slovakia at 17.2%, still anchored to coal, gas and nuclear. The clean-power shift is also a fiscal story: Eurostat calculates the EU avoided €51.4 billion in fossil-fuel imports in 2025 by generating more of its own electricity, a savings figure that has sharpened after three years of energy-security shocks tied to the war in Ukraine.

Christopher Weafer, chief executive of consultancy Macro-Advisory, described an economy "under increasing stress" as the fuel shortages reach ordinary consumers far from the front.

Zelenskyy framed the crisis as deliberate leverage, saying Russians are being made to "feel the consequences of its own aggression" — a reversal of the energy weapon Moscow wielded against Europe in 2022, now turned back on the Russian home front.

### UN expert panel warns the window to govern AI is closing and could widen global inequality

The UN's newly formed Independent International Scientific Panel on AI warned that the window to steer artificial intelligence toward the public good is narrowing, and that unchecked development could entrench a vast gap between a handful of dominant states and everyone else. The 40-member body, established by the General Assembly in 2025, issued its warning as compute power concentrates in fewer hands.

The concentration numbers are stark: the United States controls roughly 75% of global AI supercomputer capacity and China about 15%, leaving the rest of the world dependent on infrastructure it does not own. The panel pointed to the EU AI Act as a rare example of binding regulation, while most jurisdictions still lack enforceable rules.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres framed the stakes bluntly. "The more AI advances without shared rules, the less say governments and people will have in the outcome," he said, pressing for international coordination before the technology's trajectory becomes impossible to redirect.

### EU's top court rejects Google's appeal and confirms the €4.1 billion Android fine

The European Court of Justice dismissed Google's appeal on July 2 and confirmed a €4.1 billion penalty over the Android operating system, ending one of the longest-running antitrust battles in EU history. The ruling upholds the finding that Google and Alphabet abused Android's dominance to entrench the company's search engine on mobile devices.

"The Court of Justice dismisses the appeal brought by Google and Alphabet...confirming the penalty...for their anticompetitive practices relating to the Android operating system," the court stated. The case dates to 2018, when the Commission imposed a record €4.3 billion fine for forcing manufacturers to pre-install Google Search and Chrome as a condition of licensing the Play Store. The General Court trimmed the penalty to €4.1 billion in 2022, and Wednesday's judgment closes the final avenue of appeal.

The defeat compounds Google's regulatory troubles in Europe: the company was hit with a further €2.95 billion fine in 2025 over its advertising-technology business, part of a widening EU campaign against the market power of the largest US platforms.

### EU's Entry-Exit System sows travel chaos ahead of the summer peak, with 40 million passengers at risk

The EU's new biometric Entry-Exit System (EES) is generating delays of up to five hours at external borders just as summer travel peaks, with the industry warning that some 40 million passengers and $45.4 billion in visitor spending are at risk this season. The system, rolled out in phases through 2025 and 2026, registers every non-EU traveller's fingerprints and facial image on entry and exit — replacing passport stamps but adding minutes of processing to each crossing.

The friction was predictable. When it launched in October 2025 the EES began registering biometrics for short-stay visitors across 29 European countries, and airports quickly reported checks taking dramatically longer than under the old stamp regime. World Travel and Tourism Council chief executive Gloria Guevara warned that the delays now threaten peak-season traffic across Europe's busiest air and land borders. With a companion travel-authorisation scheme still to come, the industry fears the summer bottlenecks are a preview of worse to follow unless capacity and automation catch up with the new requirements.
