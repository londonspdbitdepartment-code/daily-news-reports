### South Korea Lowers Crude Oil Security Alert to Level 2 as U.S.-Iran Tensions Ease

Vice Industry Minister Moon Shin-hak announced the government would lower its national resource security crisis warning for crude oil from Level 3 to Level 2 during a Cabinet meeting at the presidential office on Tuesday. Natural gas, which had held Level 2 status since April, will be cleared entirely. "The alert for natural gas will be fully lifted as well," Moon said.

The move unwinds an escalation ladder South Korea climbed across the spring. The government first issued the lowest crude alert in early March 2026, raised it to Level 2 later that month, then pushed it to Level 3 in April as fighting around the Strait of Hormuz threatened the chokepoint through which much of the world's oil flows. The four-tier warning system governs how aggressively Seoul manages a resource squeeze; at the top rungs it leaned on emergency measures such as fuel rotation arrangements that are now being stood down.

The downgrade tracks two outside shifts: oil prices have stabilized, and the United States and Iran returned to peace negotiations. South Korea, which imports nearly all of its crude, has been securing barrels through routes that bypass the Strait of Hormuz, including supply from the United States — insulating refiners from the worst-case disruption that the Level 3 alert was built to guard against.

### South Korea May Tax Revenue Surges 19% on Higher Incomes and a Booming Stock Market

South Korea collected 35.8 trillion won ($23.2 billion) in taxes in May 2026, a nearly 19 percent jump from 30.1 trillion won a year earlier, the Ministry of Economy and Finance reported. The surge came from two engines: rising household income and a stock market running red-hot.

Income tax brought in 22 trillion won, up 16.5 percent year-on-year, as wages and capital gains climbed. The clearest market fingerprint was the securities transaction tax, which more than quadrupled to 1.3 trillion won from just 300 billion won a year earlier — a direct read of how heavily Koreans are trading. The KOSPI has roughly doubled in 2026, drawing a wave of novice retail investors and driving turnover to levels the tax base had not seen. Corporate tax rose 9.6 percent to 7.6 trillion won.

Across January through May, cumulative tax revenue reached 199.9 trillion won, up 16 percent on the year — a cushion for a government that has pledged hundreds of trillions of won toward semiconductor and AI ambitions.

### S&P Global Raises South Korea's 2026 Growth Forecast to 3% as Earnings Concentrate in Chips

S&P Global Ratings raised South Korea's 2026 GDP growth forecast to 3 percent, citing a corporate earnings boom that is increasingly lopsided toward semiconductors. The combined operating profit of Korea's 100 largest companies reached nearly 140 trillion won ($90 billion) in the first quarter of 2026 — but the gains pooled in a handful of chip names.

"Growth is concentrated in semiconductors, while most other sectors are either growing modestly or remain under external pressure," S&P analyst Jeremy Kim said. The agency forecasts the AI-driven semiconductor supercycle will run through at least 2028, a tailwind that lifts the headline number even as exporters outside memory grapple with tariffs and soft demand.

That concentration is the same dynamic powering the equity market, where SK hynix and Samsung Electronics have driven the KOSPI to a roughly 100 percent gain on the year. S&P paired the upgrade with a caution that chipmakers should "maintain financial discipline as they expand capacity, given the cyclical nature of the industry," a hedge against the supercycle eventually turning.

### South Korea to Accelerate Nuclear Plant Construction to Power Surging AI Electricity Demand

Presidential Chief of Staff Kang Hoon-sik said the government will look to compress the timeline for building nuclear reactors, framing faster atomic power as the answer to the electricity surge coming from AI data centers. "We will examine ways to shorten that timeline," Kang said, noting that a plant in South Korea typically takes nine to 10 years to build and that the country's average construction period runs about 56 months. The details are to be set out in the forthcoming basic plan for long-term electricity supply and demand.

The push builds on a nuclear consensus that has hardened over the past year. South Korea's 11th Basic Plan for Electricity Supply and Demand, finalized in early 2025, calls for nuclear to supply 31.8 percent of generation by 2030 and 35.6 percent by 2038, lifting nuclear capacity from 24.7 gigawatts to roughly 35 gigawatts over that span. In January 2026, the Lee administration confirmed it would proceed with two new large reactors written into that plan, and a separate law clearing the way for small modular reactors passed early in the year after stalling for two years.

The urgency is electrical arithmetic. The same semiconductor and AI buildout that is drawing hundreds of trillions of won in fab and data-center investment also demands power on a scale Korea's grid was not sized for — and reactors that take a decade to build cannot come online fast enough to meet load arriving this decade. Shortening construction is the government's lever to close that gap.

Lee called the package "historic and national recompense for Honam." The timing is deliberate: Gwangju and South Jeolla Province merge into a single Jeonnam-Gwangju Special City on July 1, 2026, encompassing 3.2 million people and 159 trillion won ($104 billion) in gross regional domestic product — a new administrative unit built to anchor the chip push.

### Canada Rules Out Splitting Submarine Contract Between Hanwha and ThyssenKrupp

Canadian Defense Minister David McGuinty dismissed speculation that Ottawa might divide its submarine procurement between two builders. "If you split a fleet of any kind, you end up in many ways with compounding costs," he said, signaling the Canadian Patrol Submarine Project will go to a single winner. The CPSP, valued at roughly 60 trillion won ($39 billion) over its life, seeks up to 12 diesel-electric submarines to replace Canada's aging Victoria-class fleet.

The contest is down to two bidders: a Hanwha Ocean-led consortium offering a KSS-III variant against Germany's Thyssenkrupp Marine Systems (TKMS), pitching the Type 212CD. Both sides have leaned on industrial-benefit arguments — Hanwha claimed over 433,000 potential jobs and CA$96.3 billion in GDP impact, while TKMS cited 650,000 jobs and CA$160 billion.

Hanwha has worked to neutralize Germany's "NATO cohesion" pitch by partnering with Babcock Canada, the local arm of Britain's Babcock International, and has won public backing from the British ambassador to Korea. A win would be a landmark for South Korea's defense exporters, who have moved aggressively into Europe and beyond and are also shopping the KSS-III to Poland and the Philippines. PM Mark Carney is expected to announce the decision before the NATO summit in Ankara on July 7.

### Ruling Democratic Party Files Bill to Impose 2% Digital Services Tax on Foreign Big Tech

South Korea's ruling Democratic Party introduced legislation on June 30, 2026, to impose a digital services tax of up to 2 percent on the domestic revenue of large foreign technology firms including Google and Netflix. The bill targets companies the party says have minimized their Korean tax burden, and would mark South Korea's first targeted digital services levy aimed squarely at overseas platforms.

The move follows years of friction over how much foreign tech owes the Korean treasury. In April 2026, the Seoul Administrative Court largely sided with Netflix, canceling 68.7 billion won of a tax bill after ruling that payments to overseas affiliates counted as service fees rather than royalties — a decision both sides appealed and one that exposed how poorly existing rules capture digital revenue earned in Korea.

A 2 percent levy on local turnover would route around that gap by taxing revenue directly rather than profits booked offshore. The approach echoes digital services taxes adopted in parts of Europe and arrives as the OECD's global tax framework, signed by more than 130 jurisdictions, has been slow to deliver. It also risks reopening trade tension: Washington has previously flagged Korea's network-fee and digital-tax measures as discriminatory against U.S. firms.

### LG Electronics Creates a CEO-Level Robotics Unit for Its Physical AI Push

LG Electronics established a new Robotics Business Center reporting directly to the CEO, launching July 1, 2026, and elevating robots from a scattered set of projects into a top-tier strategic line. Song Si-yong will lead the unit, which pursues a three-pronged plan: home robots, industrial robots via Robostar, and commercial robots through Bear Robotics.

The structural move signals where LG thinks the next growth wave sits. "Physical AI" — software intelligence embedded in machines that act in the real world — has become an industry rallying cry as companies race to translate the AI boom from screens into hardware. LG plans to build a data factory for robot training at its Yangjae R&D campus, the equivalent of a fuel depot for teaching machines to move and manipulate, and intends to develop its own actuators by leaning on decades of motor expertise from its appliance business.

The bet is that LG's "One LG" approach — pulling motor, battery, sensor, and software know-how from across the conglomerate — gives it an edge over pure-play robotics startups. Putting the center under the CEO removes the layers that slow cross-division coordination.

### U.S. Class-Action DRAM Lawsuit Against Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron Seen as Minor Threat

A proposed class-action lawsuit filed in U.S. District Court for Northern California names Samsung Electronics, SK hynix, and Micron Technology, which collectively control roughly 90 percent of the global DRAM market. The 17 plaintiffs allege the three makers deliberately limited production of conventional memory products such as DDR3 and DDR4 while shifting capacity toward the high-bandwidth memory used in AI accelerators, driving up prices for ordinary memory.

The complaint describes the same supply squeeze that has powered the chipmakers' record earnings: as fabs pivot to the high-margin HBM that feeds AI accelerators, legacy DRAM grows scarce and dear. The plaintiffs argue this was coordinated; the companies' defense is that it reflects rational capacity allocation in a boom. The complaint itself concedes that total DRAM wafer capacity continues to expand, with global capacity projected to rise through 2026 even as a larger share goes to HBM — a fact that cuts against an artificial-shortage theory.

Analysts expect minimal impact on the three firms, and forecast DRAM prices will continue climbing through 2027 regardless of the suit. For Samsung and SK hynix, whose memory windfall is the engine behind both their profits and the KOSPI's surge, the legal risk reads as noise against the supercycle.

### Pohang Haenyeo Divers Protest, Demanding Compensation for Harbor Construction Damage

Fifty-two haenyeo from Homigot in Pohang protested outside Ssangyong Engineering & Construction's Seoul headquarters on June 29–30, 2026, blaming a harbor renovation project for wrecking their livelihood. "We make our living from the sea, and if there is nothing to harvest, there is nothing to earn," one diver said.

The women, traditional free divers aged from their 60s to their 80s, say the breakwater built during the project (April 2021–June 2026) choked off the marine life they depend on. Daily yields dropped from around 10 kilograms to barely 2 kilograms, cutting earnings that had run about 1 million won monthly to a fraction of that. The company has refused to pay compensation.

Haenyeo culture, recognized by UNESCO and concentrated on Jeju and the southeastern coast, is already aging toward extinction as few young women take up the punishing work. For a community where most divers are well past retirement age, the loss of a fishing ground is not a temporary setback but the end of a way of earning a living — the grievance that brought them to a construction firm's lobby in the capital.

### Korean Domestic Tourism Rebounds in 2025 as 97% Take Local Trips and Spending Climbs 7.3%

97 percent of Koreans took at least one domestic trip in 2025, up 1.6 points, as travel within the country rebounded across every measure, the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism reported in its 2025 National Travel Survey published June 30, 2026. Koreans made 300.9 million domestic trips spanning 472.5 million days and spent 39.5 trillion won ($25.5 billion) — up 3.1, 5.4, and 7.3 percent respectively, with spending growing fastest of the three.

The per-person average was 6.5 trips and 10.2 days at 852,000 won. The share of trips including an overnight stay rose to 41.3 percent from 40 percent, a sign that Koreans are traveling not just more often but for longer. The 2025 figures build on a 2024 baseline of roughly 291.8 million domestic trips and 36.8 trillion won in spending, extending a post-pandemic recovery that has now pushed domestic travel past pre-COVID norms.

The figures also show how lopsided the travel ledger remains. Only 21.5 percent of Koreans traveled abroad in 2025, yet that minority spent 36.1 trillion won overseas — nearly matching the entire domestic tourism economy. The gap is the structural challenge behind the government's push to keep tourism money circulating at home rather than draining out through outbound trips.

### Samsung Electronics Union Chief Survives Confidence Vote After Backlash Over Wage Deal

Samsung Electronics Labor Union Chair Choi Seung-ho retained his position through a confidence vote held June 24–30, 2026, with 33,550 votes in favor, or 87.5 percent.
